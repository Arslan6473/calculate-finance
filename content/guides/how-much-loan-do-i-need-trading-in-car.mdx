When you trade in a car, the loan you need isn't simply the price of the new
car minus what the dealer gives you for the old one. Taxes, fees, your
down payment, and — most importantly — whether you still owe money on your
trade-in all change the amount you'll finance. This guide gives you the
formula, works through examples with positive and negative equity, and shows
how to keep the loan as small as possible.

## The Formula

```
Loan amount = Price of new car
            + Sales tax and fees
            − Trade-in value
            + Payoff on your current loan
            − Cash down payment
            − Rebates and incentives
```

Two of these terms combine into your **trade-in equity**:

```
Trade-in equity = Trade-in value − Loan payoff
```

- **Positive equity** (the car is worth more than you owe) works like an
  extra down payment and shrinks the new loan.
- **Negative equity** (you owe more than the car is worth) is added to the
  new loan unless you pay it off in cash.

## Step-by-Step Example: Positive Equity

You're buying a car with a negotiated price of **$35,000**. The numbers:

| Item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Negotiated price | $35,000 |
| Trade-in value offered | $15,000 |
| Payoff on your current loan | $12,000 |
| Sales tax (7%) | applied to $35,000 − $15,000 = $20,000 → $1,400 |
| Title, registration, and dealer fees | $800 |
| Cash down payment | $2,000 |

Your trade-in equity is $15,000 − $12,000 = **$3,000**.

```
Loan = $35,000 + $1,400 + $800 − $15,000 + $12,000 − $2,000 = $32,200
```

You need to finance **$32,200**. At 7% APR for 60 months, that's about
**$638 a month** and roughly $6,056 in total interest.

### How Sales Tax Treats Your Trade-In

Many states charge sales tax only on the difference between the new car's
price and your trade-in value, which is why the tax above applies to $20,000
rather than $35,000. That tax break is one advantage of trading in rather
than selling privately. Not every state offers it, and some cap it. In a
state that taxes the full price, the tax in this example would be $2,450 and
the loan $1,050 larger. Check your state's motor vehicle or revenue agency.

## Example: Negative Equity

Now suppose you still owe **$18,000** on the trade-in, which is worth
$15,000. Your equity is $15,000 − $18,000 = **−$3,000**.

```
Loan = $35,000 + $1,400 + $800 − $15,000 + $18,000 − $2,000 = $38,200
```

The $3,000 you were underwater on your old car is now part of your new loan.
Compared with the positive-equity example, you'd borrow $6,000 more:

| Scenario | Loan amount | Payment (7%, 60 months) | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $3,000 positive equity | $32,200 | $638 | $6,056 |
| $3,000 negative equity | $38,200 | $756 | $7,184 |
| Negative equity, stretched to 72 months | $38,200 | $651 | $8,692 |

Stretching the loan to 72 months brings the payment back near the original
level, but it costs about $2,600 more in interest than the positive-equity
loan, and it makes it more likely you'll be underwater again on the new car.

![Bar chart of the loan needed for a $35,000 car: $32,200 with $3,000 of positive trade-in equity and $38,200 with $3,000 of negative equity](/images/guides/how-much-loan-do-i-need-trading-in-car-scenarios.svg "Negative equity on a trade-in is added to the new loan.")

The Federal Trade Commission warns that some dealers promise to "pay off
your trade-in no matter what you owe," then add the negative equity to the
new loan or subtract it from your down payment. Either way, you still pay
it — with interest. Check the **amount financed** on the contract before you
sign.

## How to Find Each Number

- **Negotiated price.** Negotiate the new car's price separately from your
  trade-in, so a generous trade-in offer isn't offset by a higher price.
- **Trade-in value.** Check valuation guides such as Kelley Blue Book,
  Edmunds, or J.D. Power (NADA), and get offers from more than one dealer or
  an online buyer.
- **Loan payoff.** Ask your current lender for a **payoff quote**, which
  includes interest through a specific date. It's usually a little more than
  the balance on your statement.
- **Taxes and fees.** Ask the dealer for an out-the-door price, which lists
  sales tax, title, registration, and documentation fees.
- **Rebates.** Manufacturer cash rebates reduce the amount you finance.
  Sometimes you have to choose between a rebate and special low-rate
  financing.

## How Much Should You Borrow?

The formula gives the *minimum* you need to borrow for this deal. Whether it's
a sensible amount is a different question:

- **Watch the loan-to-value.** If the loan is larger than the new car is
  worth, you start out underwater. Many lenders cap how much they'll lend
  relative to the car's value, which can limit how much negative equity you
  can roll in.
- **Keep the term short.** Cars lose value quickly, and longer loans keep
  your balance above the car's value for longer.
- **Consider GAP coverage if you're underwater.** If the car is totaled,
  insurance pays its value, not your loan balance. Guaranteed asset
  protection covers the gap — but compare prices, because it's often
  cheaper from an insurer than from the dealer.
- **Fit the payment into your budget.** A common guideline is to keep total
  car costs — payment, insurance, fuel, and maintenance — to a modest share of
  take-home pay. Lenders will also count the payment in your
  [debt-to-income ratio](/guides/good-debt-to-income-ratio).

## Ways to Reduce the Loan

- **Increase the down payment,** even by a few thousand dollars.
- **Pay off negative equity first.** Paying down your current loan before
  trading in, or making a few extra principal payments, keeps the old debt
  out of the new loan.
- **Sell privately.** You'll often get more than a dealer's trade-in offer,
  though you may lose the sales tax credit and take on more hassle.
- **Choose a less expensive car.** The price is the single biggest number
  in the formula.
- **Wait.** If you're underwater, a few more months of payments may bring
  you to break-even.

## Putting It Together

Start with the new car's price, add taxes and fees, subtract your trade-in
value, add whatever you still owe on it, and subtract your cash down
payment and rebates. If you have positive equity, it lowers the loan. If
you have negative equity, it raises the loan, and you'll pay interest on it
again.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a trade-in count as a down payment?** Your trade-in *equity* does.
If your car is worth $15,000 and you owe $12,000, the $3,000 difference
reduces the loan the same way a cash down payment would.

**Can I trade in a car that isn't paid off?** Yes. The dealer pays off your
existing loan as part of the deal, and the payoff amount is factored into
the new loan.

**What happens if I owe more than my trade-in is worth?** The difference —
negative equity — is usually added to your new loan, unless you pay it in
cash. You'll pay interest on it over the new loan's term.

**Should I tell the dealer about my trade-in up front?** It's often better
to agree on the new car's price first, then negotiate the trade-in
separately, so you can see the value of each part of the deal.
