When you apply for a mortgage, a car loan, or a personal loan, the lender
wants to know two things: how much you earn and how much of that income is
already spoken for. Your **debt-to-income ratio** (DTI) answers both in a
single percentage. It's one of the most important numbers in a mortgage
application, and it's simple enough to calculate yourself in a few minutes.
This guide explains the formula, what counts as debt and what doesn't, how
lenders use the two different versions of the ratio, and how to lower yours
before you apply.

## The Basic Formula

Your DTI compares your recurring monthly debt payments to your gross monthly
income — what you earn before taxes and other deductions come out.

```
DTI = Total monthly debt payments ÷ Gross monthly income × 100
```

If you pay $2,000 a month toward debts and earn $6,000 a month before taxes,
your DTI is $2,000 ÷ $6,000 = **33.3%**.

Two details trip people up:

- **It uses gross income, not take-home pay.** Your paycheck after taxes,
  retirement contributions, and health insurance is smaller, but lenders
  start from the pre-tax figure.
- **It uses minimum required payments, not balances.** A $9,000 credit card
  balance enters the formula as its minimum monthly payment, not as $9,000.

## What Counts as Debt

Lenders count obligations that recur every month and appear on your credit
report or in legal documents. The usual list:

- **Housing.** Your rent, or — for a mortgage application — the proposed
  monthly payment including principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners
  insurance, mortgage insurance, and any HOA dues.
- **Auto loans and leases.**
- **Student loans**, including ones that are deferred. Fannie Mae's guidelines
  let a lender use a payment equal to 1% of the outstanding balance when the
  credit report doesn't show a usable monthly payment.
- **Credit card minimum payments**, for every card with a balance.
- **Personal loans, installment loans, and lines of credit.**
- **Court-ordered payments** such as child support and alimony.
- **Loans you co-signed**, which often count even if someone else makes the
  payments, unless you can document that they do.

Fannie Mae's underwriting rules, for example, require installment debts —
student loans, car loans, personal loans — to be counted if more than ten
monthly payments remain.

## What Doesn't Count

DTI is not a full budget. These everyday expenses are generally left out:

- Utilities, phone, and internet
- Groceries, gas, and transportation costs other than loan payments
- Health, auto, and life insurance premiums
- Subscriptions and memberships
- Income taxes and payroll deductions
- Child care (unless it's a court-ordered obligation)

That's why a "good" DTI can still feel tight. Two people with identical
ratios can have very different amounts left over at the end of the month
depending on taxes, child care, and commuting costs.

## Front-End vs. Back-End DTI

Mortgage lenders often look at two versions of the ratio.

**Front-end DTI** (also called the housing ratio) includes only your
proposed housing payment:

```
Front-end DTI = Monthly housing payment ÷ Gross monthly income × 100
```

**Back-end DTI** (the total ratio) includes housing plus every other debt
payment:

```
Back-end DTI = (Housing payment + Other debt payments) ÷ Gross monthly income × 100
```

When people say "DTI" without qualification, they almost always mean the
back-end ratio. A long-standing rule of thumb is the **28/36 rule**: keep
housing at or below 28% of gross income and total debt at or below 36%.
It's a planning guideline, not a legal limit.

## A Worked Example

Suppose you earn **$78,000 a year**. Your gross monthly income is $78,000 ÷ 12
= **$6,500**. Your current debts are:

| Debt | Monthly payment |
| --- | --- |
| Car loan | $450 |
| Student loan | $300 |
| Credit card minimums (two cards) | $120 |
| **Total non-housing debt** | **$870** |

### Today, While Renting

Your rent is $1,600. Your current back-end DTI is:

```
($1,600 + $870) ÷ $6,500 = $2,470 ÷ $6,500 = 38.0%
```

### With a Proposed Mortgage

Now suppose you're shopping for a home with an estimated payment of **$1,850**
a month, including taxes and insurance.

- **Front-end DTI:** $1,850 ÷ $6,500 = **28.5%**
- **Back-end DTI:** ($1,850 + $870) ÷ $6,500 = $2,720 ÷ $6,500 = **41.8%**

The housing ratio sits right around the 28% guideline, but the total ratio is
well above 36%. The house itself isn't the problem — the car loan, student
loan, and credit cards are taking up a large share of income before the
mortgage is even added.

### What Changes the Picture

| Change | Back-end DTI |
| --- | --- |
| As above | 41.8% |
| Pay off both credit cards | 40.0% |
| Pay off the car loan | 34.9% |

Paying off the cards drops the ratio by about two points. Paying off the
$450 car loan drops it by almost seven, bringing it under 36%. Alternatively,
keeping all existing debts, the housing payment that fits within 36% is
36% × $6,500 − $870 = **$1,470** a month, about $380 less than the home you
were considering. And to carry the $1,850 payment with every current debt at
a 36% ratio, you'd need an income of about **$90,667** a year.

## What Lenders Look For

There is no single universal cutoff, because limits depend on the loan
program, your credit score, your savings, and how the loan is underwritten.
Fannie Mae's guidelines illustrate how this works for conventional loans:

- For **manually underwritten** loans, the maximum total DTI is **36%**, and
  it can go up to **45%** if the borrower meets additional credit score and
  reserve requirements.
- For loans run through Fannie Mae's automated underwriting system, the
  maximum allowable DTI is **50%**.

Government-backed loans such as FHA and VA mortgages have their own rules,
and individual lenders can set stricter limits than the agencies require.
Being approved at a high DTI also isn't the same as being comfortable at
one. A ratio near 50% means half your pre-tax income goes to fixed payments
before you've paid for taxes, food, or anything else.

As a general guide, a back-end DTI in the low-to-mid 30s gives most
households reasonable breathing room. Above the low 40s, loan options start
to narrow and monthly budgets tend to get tight.

## How to Lower Your DTI

You can improve the ratio from either side: reduce the debt payments on top,
or raise the income on the bottom.

**Reduce monthly debt payments:**

- **Pay off small balances completely.** A paid-off card stops counting
  entirely, while a lower balance may only shave a few dollars off its
  minimum payment.
- **Target the debt with the largest payment relative to its balance.** A
  car loan with a few thousand dollars left but a $450 payment is often the
  fastest way to move the ratio.
- **Avoid new debt before applying.** Financing furniture or a car right
  before a mortgage application can push you over a limit.
- **Refinance or consolidate carefully.** A longer term lowers the monthly
  payment and the ratio, but usually increases the total interest you pay.

**Increase documented income:**

- **Include all qualifying income.** Lenders can count income from a second
  job, overtime, bonuses, or self-employment if it's stable and documented,
  usually over a two-year history.
- **Add a co-borrower.** A spouse or partner's income can lower the combined
  ratio, though their debts are added too.

**Adjust the housing side:**

- **Look at a less expensive home** or a larger down payment, both of which
  shrink the proposed payment.
- **Shop for property taxes and insurance**, which can vary meaningfully
  between neighborhoods and insurers.

## DTI Isn't Your Credit Score

Your debt-to-income ratio doesn't appear on your credit report and isn't
part of your credit score — credit reports don't include your income. Your
**credit utilization**, which compares card balances to credit limits, is a
different measure that does affect your score. Paying down cards can help
both numbers at the same time, but they're calculated separately and used
for different purposes.

## Putting It Together

Add up every required monthly debt payment — housing, cars, student loans,
card minimums, and other loans — and divide by your gross monthly income.
Look at the front-end ratio to see whether the housing payment alone is
reasonable, and the back-end ratio to see whether everything together fits.
Lenders' maximums are often far above what's comfortable, so aim for a
number you can live with, not just one you can qualify for.
