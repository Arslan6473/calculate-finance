Rent is the biggest monthly bill for most renters, which makes it the single
most important number to get right in a budget. Set it too high and every
other goal — saving, paying down debt, handling an emergency — gets squeezed.
There's no one correct figure, but there are a few well-known rules of thumb,
each answering the question from a slightly different angle. This guide
explains how each one works, where it falls short, and how to combine them
into a number that fits your actual finances.

## The 30% Rule

The most familiar guideline says to spend no more than **30% of your gross
monthly income** on rent. Gross income means pay before taxes and deductions.

```
Maximum rent = Gross monthly income × 30%
```

On a $60,000 salary, gross monthly income is $60,000 ÷ 12 = $5,000. Thirty
percent of that is **$1,500** a month.

The 30% benchmark has deep roots — it's the standard federal housing agencies
have long used to define whether a household is "cost-burdened" by housing.
It's simple, easy to remember, and a reasonable starting point.

### Where the 30% Rule Falls Short

- **It uses gross income, not take-home pay.** Two people earning $60,000 can
  take home very different amounts depending on their state taxes, health
  insurance premiums, and retirement contributions.
- **It ignores debt.** Someone with a $400 car payment and a $300 student loan
  payment has far less room for rent than someone with no debt, even at the
  same salary.
- **It doesn't scale well.** At low incomes, 30% may leave too little for
  food, transportation, and other essentials. At high incomes, 30% may be far
  more than anyone needs to spend.
- **It ignores local reality.** In expensive cities, many renters spend well
  over 30% because there's no practical alternative — and the rule doesn't
  tell you what to cut elsewhere to make that work.

Think of 30% as a ceiling to stay under when you can, not a target to spend up
to.

## The 50/30/20 Budget

The 50/30/20 framework takes a broader view. It divides your **after-tax**
income into three buckets:

- **50% for needs:** rent, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation,
  minimum debt payments.
- **30% for wants:** dining out, entertainment, travel, subscriptions.
- **20% for savings and extra debt payments:** emergency fund, retirement,
  paying down debt beyond the minimums.

Rent doesn't get its own percentage here. It shares the 50% needs bucket with
every other essential, which forces you to see it in context.

### A Worked Example

Suppose you earn $60,000 a year and take home $3,900 a month after taxes and
deductions. Half of that, **$1,950**, is your needs budget. Now list your
other needs:

| Need | Monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| Groceries | $350 |
| Utilities | $120 |
| Phone and internet | $90 |
| Transportation | $180 |
| Renters insurance | $15 |
| **Total (excluding rent)** | **$755** |

That leaves $1,950 − $755 = **$1,195** for rent.

Notice the gap. The 30% rule said this person could spend $1,500 on rent; the
50/30/20 approach says about $1,200. The difference comes from using take-home
pay instead of gross income and from accounting for other essential costs. If
this person also had a $250 minimum student loan payment, their rent budget
would fall to $945.

The 50/30/20 split is a guideline, not a rule. Plenty of people reasonably
shift a few percent from wants to needs in an expensive city. The value is in
making the tradeoff visible: every dollar of rent above your needs budget has
to come out of wants or savings.

## The Landlord's View: The 40x Rule

Many landlords and property managers screen applicants with an income
requirement, most commonly that your **gross annual income be at least 40
times the monthly rent**.

```
Maximum rent landlords will typically approve = Gross annual income ÷ 40
```

For $60,000 in income: $60,000 ÷ 40 = **$1,500**.

That matches the 30% rule exactly, and not by coincidence. Rent equal to 1/40
of annual income is 12/40 of monthly income — which is 30%. Some landlords
instead require gross monthly income of at least 3 times the rent, which works
out to 36 times rent annually — slightly looser than 40x. In competitive
markets, some ask for more.

Keep in mind what this rule is: a landlord's tool for estimating whether
you'll pay on time. Passing the screen tells you a landlord will rent to you.
It doesn't tell you the rent is a comfortable fit for your budget. Many
landlords also check your credit, and some look at your existing debts or ask
for a guarantor if your income falls short.

## Account for Your Debts

If you carry debt, adjust your rent budget for it directly. A simple approach
borrowed from mortgage lending is to look at your total housing cost plus debt
payments as a share of gross income. Mortgage lenders have long used
guidelines such as housing costs at or below about 28% of gross income and
total debt payments at or below about 36% — the "28/36" rule.

Applied to renting, that suggests that if your monthly debt payments already
take up a large share of your income, your rent should be correspondingly
lower. On $5,000 of gross monthly income, 36% is $1,800. If you pay $500 a
month toward a car loan and student loans, that leaves about $1,300 for rent
under this approach — less than the $1,500 the 30% rule alone would allow.

## The Hidden Costs of Renting

Rent is the headline number, but it's rarely the full monthly cost of a place
to live. Before signing, find out which of these apply:

- **Utilities.** Electricity, gas, water, sewer, and trash may or may not be
  included. Ask the landlord or previous tenant for typical monthly bills.
- **Internet.** Almost never included, and almost always needed.
- **Renters insurance.** Inexpensive relative to what it covers, and some
  leases require it.
- **Parking.** In many cities a parking space carries a separate monthly fee.
- **Pet fees.** Monthly pet rent, a one-time pet fee, a pet deposit, or a
  combination.
- **Amenity or service fees.** Some buildings charge monthly fees for trash
  valet, package lockers, or shared amenities.

These add up. A $1,500 apartment with $150 in utilities, $15 for renters
insurance, and $75 for parking is really a $1,740-a-month housing cost. On
$5,000 of gross monthly income, that's 34.8% — over the 30% guideline even
though the rent alone was right at it.

### Move-In Costs

Moving into a new place requires cash up front, often:

- **First month's rent**, due at signing.
- **Security deposit**, frequently equal to one month's rent. Many states cap
  how much a landlord can charge and set rules for returning it.
- **Last month's rent**, in some markets.
- **Application fees** for each place you apply to.
- **Broker fees** in some cities.
- **Moving costs**, whether a truck rental or professional movers.

For that $1,500 apartment, first month plus a one-month security deposit alone
is $3,000 — before application fees and the move itself. Make sure you can
cover move-in costs without draining your emergency fund.

## How to Set Your Own Number

Put the rules together rather than relying on any single one:

1. **Start with the 30% rule** on gross income to get a quick upper bound.
2. **Check it against your take-home pay** with the 50/30/20 approach, after
   listing your other essential costs and debt payments.
3. **Add the hidden monthly costs** — utilities, insurance, parking — to the
   rent you're considering and make sure the total still fits.
4. **Confirm you'll pass the landlord's screen**, typically 40 times the
   monthly rent in annual income.
5. **Make sure you have the cash for move-in costs** on top of an emergency
   fund.

The lowest number from steps 1 through 3 is usually the safest rent to aim
for. If it's lower than what's available in your area, that's useful
information too: it tells you how much you'd need to trim elsewhere, whether a
roommate makes sense, or whether a different neighborhood is worth a look.

## Putting It Together

The 30% rule is a fine quick check, but it doesn't see your taxes, your debts,
or the costs that come with an apartment beyond the rent. A budget built from
your take-home pay — with debts and hidden costs included — gives a more
realistic number, and the landlord's 40x rule tells you whether you'll
qualify.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it okay to spend more than 30% of your income on rent?** Many renters
do, especially in expensive cities where there's no practical alternative.
Federal housing agencies use 30% as the line for being "cost-burdened," so
going over it means less room for savings, debt payments, and emergencies.
Every dollar above your budget has to come out of wants or savings.

**How much income do I need to rent a $2,000 apartment?** Under the common
40x rule, landlords would look for gross annual income of at least $80,000.
Landlords that require monthly income of 3 times the rent would look for
$6,000 a month, or $72,000 a year. Requirements vary by landlord, and some
ask for more in competitive markets.

**Is the 30% rent rule based on gross or net income?** Gross income, before
taxes and deductions. Budgeting from take-home pay usually gives a lower
number: in this guide's example, $60,000 a year supports $1,500 under the 30%
rule but about $1,195 under a 50/30/20 budget. The [rent affordability
calculator](/calculators/rent-affordability-calculator) shows both views.

**How much money do I need to move into an apartment?** Often at least the
first month's rent plus a security deposit, which is frequently equal to one
month's rent — $3,000 on a $1,500 apartment. Some markets also require last
month's rent or broker fees, and application fees and moving costs come on
top. Many states cap security deposits, so the amount varies by location.
