Credit card interest feels opaque on purpose. Your statement shows an APR,
a balance, and an interest charge, but not how one number turned into the
other. Once you see the mechanics — a daily rate, an average balance, and a
grace period that can quietly disappear — the charges stop being mysterious,
and it becomes much clearer why carrying a balance is so expensive and why
paying only the minimum can take decades. This guide walks through each piece
with real numbers.

## APR: The Starting Point

Every credit card has an **annual percentage rate (APR)**. For most cards,
the APR is the interest rate expressed on a yearly basis; unlike mortgage
APRs, it doesn't fold in fees. A single card often has several APRs at once:

- **Purchase APR** for everyday spending.
- **Balance transfer APR**, sometimes a low promotional rate for a limited
  time.
- **Cash advance APR**, usually higher than the purchase rate, with interest
  that typically starts immediately.
- **Penalty APR**, which may apply after a late payment.

Most card APRs are **variable**, meaning they're tied to an index — commonly
the prime rate — plus a fixed margin. When the index moves, your rate follows,
as described in your card agreement.

Rates are high relative to other borrowing. The Federal Reserve's G.19
consumer credit release, which reports average rates at commercial banks, has
shown average rates above 20% on credit card accounts assessed interest in its
recent releases. The examples below use a 24% APR for round numbers.

## From APR to a Daily Periodic Rate

Card issuers don't charge interest once a year. Most calculate it daily,
using a **daily periodic rate (DPR)**: the APR divided by 365 (some issuers
use 360).

For a 24% APR:

24% ÷ 365 = **0.06575% per day**

That looks tiny, but it applies to every dollar of your balance every day of
the billing cycle.

## The Average Daily Balance Method

The most common way to calculate the interest charge is the **average daily
balance** method. The issuer records your balance at the end of each day in
the billing cycle, adds those daily balances together, and divides by the
number of days. Then:

Interest = average daily balance × daily periodic rate × days in the cycle

### A Worked Example

Say you start a 30-day billing cycle with a **$2,000** balance carried from
the previous month, and you're not in a grace period:

- **Days 1–10:** The balance is $2,000.
- **Day 11:** You make a $500 purchase. The balance is $2,500 for days 11–20.
- **Day 21:** You make a $300 payment. The balance is $2,200 for days 21–30.

The average daily balance is:

(10 × $2,000 + 10 × $2,500 + 10 × $2,200) ÷ 30 = $67,000 ÷ 30 =
**$2,233.33**

The interest charge for the cycle is:

$2,233.33 × 0.0006575 × 30 = **$44.05**

Two details stand out. First, the $500 purchase started costing interest the
day it posted, because there was no grace period on a card carrying a
balance. Second, timing matters: had the $300 payment been made on day 1
instead of day 21, the average balance — and the interest — would have been
lower.

Many issuers also **compound daily**, adding each day's interest to the
balance used for the next day. In this example, daily compounding raises the
charge to about **$44.47** — a small difference in one month, but it's part of
why a 24% APR costs slightly more than 24% over a full year. A $1,000 balance
compounded daily at 24% for a year accrues about $271 in interest, not $240.

## The Grace Period: How to Pay Zero Interest

A **grace period** is the window between the end of a billing cycle and the
payment due date. If you pay your statement balance **in full** by the due
date, most cards charge no interest on new purchases. Federal rules require
issuers to deliver your statement at least 21 days before the payment due
date, so you get at least three weeks to pay.

The catch is how easily the grace period is lost:

- **Carry any balance past the due date** and, on most cards, new purchases
  start accruing interest from the day they post — as in the example above.
- **Getting it back** usually requires paying the full statement balance for
  one or sometimes two consecutive cycles, depending on the card's terms.
- **Residual (trailing) interest** can appear even after you pay in full,
  because interest accrued between the statement date and the day your payment
  posted. Expect a small charge on the next statement.
- **Cash advances and balance transfers** often have no grace period at all.

For a cardholder who always pays in full, the APR is effectively irrelevant.
For anyone carrying a balance, it determines the cost of nearly everything.

## The Minimum Payment Trap

Card issuers set a **minimum payment** that keeps your account in good
standing. A common formula is **1% of the balance plus that month's
interest**, with a floor of around $25. Because the minimum shrinks as the
balance shrinks, paying only the minimum keeps you in debt for a very long
time.

### Running the Numbers

Take a **$5,000** balance at a **24% APR** (2% per month for simplicity), no
new charges, and a minimum of 1% of the balance plus interest, with a $25
floor:

- **First month:** Interest is $5,000 × 2% = $100. The minimum is $50 + $100 =
  **$150**, and only $50 of it reduces the balance.
- **As the balance falls,** the required payment falls with it, so the payoff
  slows down rather than speeding up.

Here's how three payment strategies compare:

| Strategy | Time to pay off | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minimum payment only | 234 months (19.5 years) | ≈ $8,887 |
| Fixed $150 a month | 56 months (4.7 years) | ≈ $3,322 |
| Fixed $200 a month | 36 months (3 years) | ≈ $2,001 |

Paying only the minimum costs nearly **$8,900 in interest** — more than the
original $5,000 balance — and stretches over almost two decades. Simply
locking in the first month's $150 payment and never letting it drop cuts the
timeline to under five years and saves about $5,565. Bumping it to $200 a
month clears the card in three years.

This is why your statement includes a minimum payment warning: federal rules
require issuers to show how long payoff would take and how much you'd pay
in total if you made only minimum payments, along with the monthly amount
needed to pay off the balance in three years.

## How Payments Are Applied

If your card has balances at different rates — say, purchases at 24% and a
cash advance at 29% — the order in which your payment is applied matters.
Issuers can apply the minimum payment however their terms allow, often to the
lowest-rate balance first. But any amount you pay **above the minimum** must go
to the balance with the highest interest rate, with special exceptions for
deferred interest plans. That's one more reason to pay more than the minimum.

## Ways to Pay Less Interest

- **Pay the statement balance in full** whenever you can to keep the grace
  period.
- **Pay early or more than once a month.** Lowering your balance earlier in
  the cycle lowers your average daily balance.
- **Pay a fixed amount** instead of the shrinking minimum. As the table shows,
  this is one of the most effective changes you can make.
- **Ask for a lower rate.** Issuers sometimes agree, especially for customers
  with a strong payment history.
- **Consider a balance transfer or consolidation loan** — but read the
  transfer fee, how long the promotional rate lasts, and what rate applies
  afterward, and avoid running the old card back up.
- **Avoid cash advances,** which usually carry a higher APR, an upfront fee,
  and no grace period.
- **Prioritize the highest-rate card** if you have several, or follow a
  structured method like the debt avalanche or snowball.

## Putting It Together

Credit card interest comes down to a few mechanics: the APR is converted to a
daily rate, that rate is applied to your average daily balance, and the
grace period lets you avoid interest entirely — but only if you pay the full
statement balance every month. Once you carry a balance, interest applies to
new purchases right away, and minimum payments that shrink with your balance
can keep you paying for decades.
