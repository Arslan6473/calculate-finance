A new purchase on your credit card doesn't necessarily start costing interest
the day you make it. Whether — and when — interest starts depends on one
thing above all: whether you paid your last statement balance in full. This
guide explains the grace period, how many interest-free days you really get,
what happens when you lose the grace period, and the transactions that start
charging interest immediately.

## The Short Answer

- **If you pay your full statement balance by the due date every month,**
  interest on purchases usually never starts. The grace period covers them.
- **If you carry a balance from last month,** interest on new purchases
  usually starts **the day each purchase posts**.
- **Cash advances** (and often balance transfers outside a promotion)
  usually start accruing interest **immediately**, with no grace period.

## What a Grace Period Is

A **grace period** is the time between the end of a billing cycle and the
payment due date during which you can pay off purchases without being
charged interest. Most cards offer one on purchases, but it isn't required by
law — check your card agreement, which says whether you have one and how it
works.

When a card does have a grace period, federal rules require the issuer to
deliver or mail your statement at least **21 days** before the payment due
date. Many cards give about 21 to 25 days.

### How Many Interest-Free Days You Actually Get

Because the grace period runs from the statement date, the total time
between a purchase and the payment due date depends on where in the cycle
you buy. Take a card with a 30-day billing cycle and a due date 25 days after
the statement closes:

| Purchase made on | Days until the due date |
| --- | --- |
| Day 1 of the billing cycle | 54 days |
| Day 15 of the billing cycle | 40 days |
| Day 30 (the statement closing date) | 25 days |

A purchase early in the cycle can go nearly two months without interest; one
the day the statement closes gets less than four weeks. Every one of them is
interest-free if the statement balance is paid in full by the due date.

## When Interest Starts on Purchases

### If You Pay in Full

You pay the full statement balance by the due date. No interest is charged
on those purchases, and the grace period continues for next month's
purchases too.

Paying the **statement balance** is what counts — not the current balance
that includes purchases made after the statement closed. Those newer
purchases have their own grace period that runs to the next due date.

### If You Pay Less Than the Full Balance

On most cards, carrying any part of the statement balance past the due date
means:

- **The unpaid balance starts accruing interest,** typically back to the
  date of each purchase rather than the due date.
- **New purchases lose the grace period** and accrue interest from the day
  they post.

There's a limit on how far back the issuer can reach. Under the federal
rule against **double-cycle billing**, a card issuer can't charge interest
that results from losing a grace period on balances from billing cycles
before the most recent one, or on any part of a balance you repaid before
the grace period ended.

**Example:** You make a $1,000 purchase on day 5 of a 30-day cycle at a 24%
APR. The due date is day 55. You pay $500 by the due date. The $500 you
repaid can't be charged interest, but the unpaid $500 accrues interest from
the purchase date: about $500 × (0.24 ÷ 365) × 50 days ≈ **$16.44** through
the due date — and it keeps accruing until you pay it off.

### Residual Interest After You Pay in Full

If you were carrying a balance and then pay the statement in full, you may
still see a small interest charge on the next statement. That's **residual**
(or trailing) interest: interest that accrued between the statement closing
date and the day your payment arrived. Paying in full again on the next
statement usually clears it.

## How to Get Your Grace Period Back

Once you lose it, most cards restore the grace period after you pay the
**full statement balance by the due date** for one billing cycle; some
require two consecutive cycles. Your card agreement says which. Until then,
new purchases accrue interest from the day they post.

## Transactions That Start Charging Interest Right Away

| Transaction | When interest usually starts |
| --- | --- |
| Purchases (paid in full each month) | Never, thanks to the grace period |
| Purchases (carrying a balance) | The day the purchase posts |
| Cash advances, ATM withdrawals | Immediately, plus a cash advance fee |
| Cash-like purchases (some money transfers, gambling, cryptocurrency) | Often treated as cash advances |
| Balance transfers (no promotion) | Usually immediately, plus a transfer fee |
| Balance transfers (0% promotion) | When the promotional period ends |

**Deferred-interest promotions** — common at retail stores, often described
as "no interest if paid in full in 12 months" — work differently from a 0%
APR. Interest builds up in the background from the purchase date, and if any
of the promotional balance is left when the period ends, you're charged all
of it.

## Does Interest Start on a New Card?

On a brand-new card with no balance, you begin with a grace period on
purchases. If the card has a 0% introductory APR, no interest is charged on
the covered balances until the promotion ends — but you still have to make
at least the minimum payment each month, and a late payment can end the
promotion early, depending on the terms.

## Putting It Together

Interest on credit card purchases starts only when you don't pay the
statement balance in full. Pay in full by the due date and the grace period
keeps purchases interest-free, giving you anywhere from three weeks to nearly two months
depending on when you buy. Carry a balance, and new purchases start
accruing interest the day they post until you've paid in full again. Cash
advances skip the grace period entirely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do you get charged interest if you pay the minimum on time?** Yes, if you
carry a balance. Paying the minimum on time avoids a late fee and keeps the
account in good standing, but interest accrues on the unpaid balance.

**Is interest charged from the purchase date or the statement date?** If
you've lost the grace period, interest is usually charged from the purchase
or posting date. If you have a grace period and pay in full, no interest is
charged.

**How long is a credit card grace period?** At least 21 days from when the
statement is delivered or mailed to the due date, for cards that offer one.
Many cards use 21 to 25 days.

**Do I pay interest if I pay my balance before the statement closes?** No.
Paying before the statement closes reduces or eliminates the statement
balance, so there's nothing left to charge interest on.
