The two most common mortgage terms in the US are 30 years and 15 years. A
30-year loan spreads the balance over twice as many payments, so each one is
smaller. A 15-year loan costs more each month but usually comes with a lower
interest rate and builds equity far faster, which can cut the total interest
you pay by more than half. Neither is automatically the right choice. This
guide compares the two side by side with real numbers, explains where the
savings come from, and walks through the questions that decide which term
fits your finances.

## Why the Term Changes So Much

A mortgage payment has to cover the interest that accrues each month plus
enough principal to bring the balance to zero by the end of the term. With a
shorter term, more principal has to be repaid each month, so the payment is
higher. But a faster-shrinking balance also means less interest accrues each
month, and that effect compounds over the life of the loan.

There's a second, separate advantage: lenders typically charge a **lower
interest rate on 15-year loans** than on 30-year loans. A shorter loan is less
exposed to long-term rate and credit risk. Freddie Mac's weekly survey of
national mortgage rates tracks both terms, and the 15-year average has
generally run below the 30-year average.

So the 15-year loan saves interest two ways: a smaller balance for less time,
and a lower rate on that balance.

## A Side-by-Side Example

Suppose you're borrowing **$300,000**. The rates below are examples for
illustration — check current rates before you decide.

- **30-year fixed** at **7.00%**
- **15-year fixed** at **6.25%**

| | 30-year | 15-year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly principal and interest | $1,995.91 | $2,572.27 |
| Number of payments | 360 | 180 |
| Total paid | $718,527 | $463,008 |
| Total interest | $418,527 | $163,008 |

The 15-year payment is **$576.36 higher** each month — about 29% more. In
exchange, total interest falls by **$255,518**, and the loan is paid off 15
years sooner.

On the 30-year loan, you'd pay more in interest ($418,527) than you borrowed.
On the 15-year loan, interest comes to a bit more than half the loan amount.

## How Fast Equity Builds

The difference in equity is just as striking. Here's the remaining balance
on each loan over time:

| After | 30-year balance | 15-year balance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 year | $296,953 | $287,530 |
| 5 years | $282,395 | $229,094 |
| 10 years | $257,437 | $132,255 |
| 15 years | $222,057 | $0 |

After five years, the 30-year borrower has paid down only about $17,600 of
principal while paying roughly $102,000 in interest. The 15-year borrower has
paid down nearly $71,000. That gap matters if you sell, refinance, or need to
borrow against your home: more equity means more options, and it provides a
cushion if home prices dip.

![Line chart of the remaining balance on a $300,000 mortgage: the 15-year loan reaches zero in year 15 while the 30-year loan falls slowly until year 30](/images/guides/15-vs-30-year-mortgage-balance.svg "The 15-year balance falls much faster from the very first year.")

## The Case for a 30-Year Mortgage

The lower payment is not just a convenience. It has real advantages:

- **Easier qualification.** A lower payment means a lower debt-to-income
  ratio, which can be the difference between approval and denial, or between
  two different homes.
- **Budget flexibility.** If your income drops or an expense spikes, a
  smaller required payment is easier to keep up with.
- **Freedom to prepay.** Many mortgages let you pay extra principal whenever
  you like. With a 30-year loan you can choose to pay as if it were a
  shorter loan in good months and fall back to the required payment in tight
  ones.
- **Room for other goals.** The $576 difference in the example could go to
  retirement accounts, an emergency fund, or paying off higher-interest debt.

### Paying a 30-Year Loan Like a 15-Year Loan

What if you take the 30-year loan but voluntarily pay the 15-year amount of
$2,572.27 every month? Because the 30-year loan's rate is higher, it doesn't
quite match the 15-year results:

| | True 15-year | 30-year paid aggressively |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payoff | 180 months | 197 months |
| Total interest | $163,008 | $204,371 |

You'd finish in about 16½ years and pay roughly **$41,000 more** in interest
than with the actual 15-year loan — the price of keeping the option to lower
your payment whenever you need to. For some households, that flexibility is
well worth it.

## The Case for a 15-Year Mortgage

- **Far less interest.** In the example, the savings exceed a quarter of a
  million dollars.
- **Built-in discipline.** The required payment forces fast payoff even when
  you'd be tempted to spend the difference.
- **Debt-free sooner.** Owning your home outright by your 50s or early 60s
  can make retirement much easier to fund, since housing is often the
  largest expense.
- **Faster equity**, which reduces the risk of owing more than the home is
  worth.

## The "Invest the Difference" Argument

A common argument for the 30-year loan is to take the lower payment and
invest the difference. Whether that works depends on returns you can't know
in advance.

In the example, investing the $576.36 monthly difference for 15 years at an
assumed **6% annual return** (compounded monthly, before taxes) would grow to
about **$167,617**. But at that point, the 30-year borrower still owes
**$222,057**, while the 15-year borrower owes nothing. In this case the
30-year borrower is roughly $54,000 behind.

For investing the difference to come out ahead, your after-tax investment
returns generally need to beat your mortgage rate — and you need to actually
invest the money every month rather than spend it. Investment returns are
uncertain; mortgage interest savings are guaranteed. When mortgage rates are
low and you're investing in tax-advantaged accounts, the math is closer;
when rates are high, paying down the mortgage looks better.

## Taxes

Mortgage interest is deductible only if you itemize deductions on your
federal return, and many households take the standard deduction instead. If
you do itemize, the deduction reduces the effective cost of interest
somewhat, which slightly narrows the gap between the two terms. It rarely
changes the conclusion: you don't come out ahead by paying a dollar of
interest to save a fraction of a dollar in tax. IRS Publication 936 explains
who can deduct mortgage interest and the limits that apply.

## How to Decide

Ask yourself these questions:

1. **Can you afford the 15-year payment comfortably** — not just qualify for
   it — while still saving for retirement and keeping an emergency fund?
2. **How stable is your income?** Variable or commission-based income makes
   the flexibility of a lower required payment more valuable.
3. **Do you have higher-interest debt?** Paying off a credit card charging
   20% or more beats prepaying a mortgage at 6% or 7%.
4. **Are you capturing any employer retirement match?** Skipping a match to
   fund a higher mortgage payment usually costs more than it saves.
5. **How long will you stay?** If you'll likely sell within a few years, the
   faster equity of a 15-year loan matters less than your monthly cash flow.

If you answer yes to the first question and your other financial goals are
on track, a 15-year loan is hard to beat. If the higher payment would stretch
you, a 30-year loan with voluntary extra payments gives you much of the
benefit with a safety valve.

## Other Terms Exist

Lenders also offer 10-, 20-, and 25-year fixed-rate loans. A 20-year term can
be a middle ground: a payment between the two, with a large share of the
interest savings. And if you already have a 30-year loan, refinancing into a
shorter term later is an option, though it comes with closing costs and
depends on the rates available at that time.

## Putting It Together

A 15-year mortgage has a higher payment but a lower rate and a much faster
payoff, which together can cut total interest by more than half. A 30-year
mortgage costs far more over its life but keeps the required payment low and
leaves room for other priorities. If the 15-year payment fits comfortably
alongside your savings goals, it's usually the cheaper path; if not, a
30-year loan with extra payments when you can afford them is a sensible
compromise.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much higher is a 15-year mortgage payment?** It depends on the loan
amount and the two rates. In this guide's example, a $300,000 loan costs
$2,572.27 a month over 15 years at 6.25%, versus $1,995.91 over 30 years at
7.00% — $576.36, or about 29%, more.

**Is it harder to qualify for a 15-year mortgage?** It can be. The higher
payment raises your debt-to-income ratio for the same loan amount, so some
borrowers qualify for a smaller loan on a 15-year term. Limits vary by lender
and loan program. The [home affordability
calculator](/calculators/home-affordability-calculator) shows how each term
changes the price you can afford.

**Can I switch from a 30-year to a 15-year mortgage later?** Yes, by
refinancing into a shorter term, which comes with closing costs and depends on
the rates available at the time. Another option is to keep the 30-year loan
and make extra principal payments, which shortens the payoff without
changing the required payment.

**Is there a penalty for paying off a mortgage early?** Many mortgages have no
prepayment penalty, and federal rules restrict them on most loans made today.
Whether your loan has one is stated on its Loan Estimate and Closing
Disclosure. Check before making large extra payments.
