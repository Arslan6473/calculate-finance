Two people can apply for the same credit card on the same day and get APRs
several percentage points apart. Your rate isn't arbitrary, though. On most
cards it's built from two parts: a benchmark rate that moves with the
economy, and a margin the issuer sets based largely on you. This guide
explains both parts, the factors that decide your margin, why card rates
change over time, and what limits apply to how — and when — an issuer can
raise them.

## The Formula Behind Most Card Rates

Most credit cards have a **variable APR**:

```
APR = Index rate + Margin
```

- **The index** is almost always the **prime rate** — the benchmark rate banks
  use for many consumer loans. By convention, the prime rate is set at 3
  percentage points above the top of the Federal Reserve's target range for
  the federal funds rate, so it moves when the Fed moves.
- **The margin** is a fixed number of percentage points the issuer adds on
  top. It's set when your account is opened and listed in your card
  agreement, often in a line like "Prime Rate + 14.99%."

Federal rules require a variable rate to be tied to an index the issuer
doesn't control, like the prime rate. The issuer controls the margin.

### Example

If the prime rate is **7.50%** and your margin is **14.99%**:

```
APR = 7.50% + 14.99% = 22.49%
```

If the Fed raises rates and the prime rate rises to 8.00%, your APR rises to
22.99% automatically — usually starting with the next billing cycle, as
described in your agreement.

## How the Prime Rate Moves Your APR

Because the index changes with Fed policy, card rates rise and fall with the
economy even when nothing about you changes. During 2020 and 2021, the prime
rate sat at **3.25%**. After the Fed raised its target range eleven times in
2022 and 2023, the prime rate reached **8.50%** — adding more than five
percentage points to every card tied to it.

Here's how the same three illustrative margins translate into APRs at those
two prime rates:

| Margin (illustrative) | APR at 3.25% prime | APR at 8.50% prime |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 11 points (excellent credit) | 14.25% | 19.50% |
| 15 points (good credit) | 18.25% | 23.50% |
| 19 points (fair credit) | 22.25% | 27.50% |

## What Determines Your Margin

The margin is where issuers price risk and make most of their profit. It has
also been rising: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found that the
average APR margin over prime reached an all-time high of about **14.3
percentage points** in 2023, and that higher margins explained nearly half
of the increase in average APRs over the prior decade.

### Your Credit Profile

The biggest factor is how likely the issuer thinks you are to repay:

- **Credit score.** Higher scores generally get lower margins. Many cards
  list an APR *range* — such as "19.24% to 28.24%" — and place you in it
  based on your credit.
- **Credit history.** Late payments, collections, high balances relative to
  your limits, and a short history all push the margin up.
- **Income and existing debt.** Issuers consider your ability to pay, which
  affects both your limit and sometimes your rate.

### The Type of Card

- **Rewards and travel cards** often carry higher APRs to help pay for the
  rewards.
- **Low-rate or "no-frills" cards** trade rewards for a smaller margin.
- **Secured cards and cards for limited credit** tend to have higher rates.
- **Store cards** often have some of the highest APRs.
- **Credit union cards** tend to offer lower rates, partly because federal
  credit unions are subject to an interest rate ceiling.

### The Issuer's Own Strategy

Issuers compete differently. Some target customers who pay in full and care
about rewards; others target customers who carry balances. That's why
margins for similar borrowers can differ meaningfully from one issuer to
the next.

## Different Rates for Different Balances

A single card usually has several APRs, each with its own margin:

- **Purchase APR** — the standard rate on everyday spending.
- **Balance transfer APR** — often a 0% or low introductory rate for a set
  period, then the standard rate.
- **Cash advance APR** — usually higher than the purchase APR, with no grace
  period.
- **Penalty APR** — a higher rate that may apply after a late payment.
- **Introductory APR** — a promotional rate, often 0%, on purchases or
  transfers for a limited time.

All of these appear in the rate and fee table that comes with the card offer
and your agreement.

## Rules That Limit Rate Increases

Federal rules restrict when an issuer can raise your rate on an existing
account:

- **During the first year,** an issuer generally can't raise your APR, except
  when a variable rate's index rises, a promotional rate ends as disclosed,
  or you're more than 60 days late.
- **Promotional rates must last at least six months.**
- **Advance notice.** For most rate increases that aren't caused by the index,
  the issuer must give you **45 days' notice**, and you generally have the
  right to reject the change by closing the account and paying off the
  balance under the old terms.
- **Existing balances are protected.** A higher rate usually applies only to
  new transactions. The issuer can raise the rate on existing balances mainly
  if you're more than **60 days late**.
- **Penalty rates must be reviewed.** If a penalty APR was triggered by a late
  payment, the issuer must reduce it after six consecutive months of on-time
  payments.

There's no general federal cap on credit card interest rates. Most large
issuers are national banks that can apply the rate rules of the state where
they're based to customers nationwide.

## How to Get a Lower Rate

- **Improve your credit.** Pay on time, lower your balances relative to your
  limits, and avoid opening several accounts at once.
- **Ask your issuer.** A customer with a solid payment history can sometimes
  get the margin lowered with a phone call.
- **Compare low-rate cards** if you tend to carry a balance; rewards rarely
  outweigh interest.
- **Check credit unions,** which often offer lower APRs.
- **Use a 0% balance transfer** to pause interest while paying down debt —
  after accounting for the transfer fee.
- **Pay in full each month.** With a grace period, the APR doesn't apply to
  purchases at all.

## Putting It Together

Most credit card APRs equal the prime rate plus a margin. The prime rate
follows the Federal Reserve and moves every card's rate up or down together.
The margin is set by the issuer based on your credit, the type of card, and
its own strategy, and it's the part you can influence. Federal rules limit
how and when that rate can rise on an existing account, but they don't cap
the rate itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why is my credit card interest rate so high?** Card rates combine the prime
rate with a margin that issuers have increased over the past decade.
Unsecured credit is riskier for lenders than a mortgage or car loan, so
margins are high, and a lower credit score raises them further.

**Does my credit card rate change when the Fed changes rates?** If your card
has a variable rate tied to the prime rate, yes. The prime rate typically
moves the same amount as the Fed's target, and your APR follows, usually
within a billing cycle or two.

**Can a credit card company raise my rate without notice?** Generally not.
Most increases require 45 days' notice. Increases caused by the index
rising, a disclosed promotional rate ending, or a payment more than 60 days
late follow separate rules described in your agreement.

**What is a good credit card APR?** Anything well below the average for
cards assessed interest is good. The Federal Reserve's G.19 release reports
the current average.
