"How much do I need to retire?" is one of the most common money questions,
and one of the hardest to answer with a single number. The honest answer
depends on how much you'll spend, when you'll stop working, how long
retirement lasts, what other income you'll have, and how your investments
perform. You can't control all of those — but you can build a reasonable
estimate, understand the assumptions behind it, and adjust as you go. This
guide walks through the most common approaches, with a full worked example.

## Start With Spending, Not a Magic Number

You'll see round-number targets thrown around — a million dollars, ten times
your salary — but the number that matters is built from **what you'll spend
each year in retirement**. Everything else follows from that.

There are two common ways to estimate it.

### The Income Replacement Approach

A widely used rule of thumb is that retirees need roughly **70% to 80% of
their pre-retirement income** to maintain a similar lifestyle. The logic is
that several big costs typically drop or disappear in retirement:

- You're no longer saving for retirement.
- Payroll taxes for Social Security and Medicare stop when wages stop.
- Commuting and work-related costs fall.
- Many people have paid off a mortgage by then.

Other costs can rise, especially health care and, for some people, travel in
the early years. The replacement ratio is a starting point, not a rule — some
people spend more in retirement than they did while working.

### The Expense-Based Approach

The more accurate method is to estimate your actual retirement budget. Start
with your current spending, then adjust line by line: remove costs that will
end, add costs that will begin (like health insurance premiums before
Medicare eligibility at 65, or long-term care), and account for goals like
travel or helping family. If you're within 10 to 15 years of retiring, this
approach is worth the effort.

## Subtract Guaranteed Income

Your savings don't need to cover your whole budget — only the part that other
income doesn't. For most Americans, the largest source of other income is
**Social Security**.

Your benefit depends on your earnings history and the age you start
collecting. You can start as early as 62, but your monthly benefit is
permanently reduced if you claim before your full retirement age, and it
increases for each year you wait, up to age 70. After 70, there's no further
increase from waiting. The best way to get your number is to create a **my
Social Security** account at the Social Security Administration's website,
which shows personalized estimates at different claiming ages based on your
actual earnings record.

Other sources of guaranteed or predictable income include:

- **Pensions** from current or former employers.
- **Annuities** you purchase.
- **Rental income** or part-time work, if you plan on it.

Subtract these from your annual spending need to find the **gap** your
savings must fill.

## Turn the Gap Into a Savings Target

Once you know the annual gap, you need a way to translate it into a total
nest egg. The most common shortcut is the **4% rule**.

### The 4% Rule

The 4% rule comes from research by financial planner William Bengen,
published in 1994, and later studies such as the "Trinity study." Using
historical U.S. stock and bond returns, Bengen found that a retiree who
withdrew 4% of a stock-and-bond portfolio in the first year of retirement,
then adjusted that dollar amount for inflation each year after, would have
seen the money last at least 30 years in every historical period he
examined. Later research reached broadly similar conclusions for balanced
portfolios over 30-year horizons.

Turned around, the rule gives you a simple multiplier:

Savings target = annual gap ÷ 0.04 = annual gap × 25

That's why you'll often hear the advice to save **25 times your annual
expenses** (net of other income).

### Important Caveats

The 4% rule is a research-based guideline, not a guarantee:

- **It's based on the past.** Future returns, inflation, and interest rates
  may be better or worse than the historical periods studied.
- **It assumes about 30 years.** Retiring early, or living well into your 90s,
  stretches the money over a longer period, which generally calls for a lower
  withdrawal rate.
- **It assumes a particular portfolio mix.** A very conservative portfolio
  may not support 4% withdrawals over 30 years; a very aggressive one may
  swing more than you can tolerate.
- **Sequence of returns matters.** A severe market drop in the first few
  years of retirement does far more damage than the same drop later, because
  you're selling investments while prices are low.
- **Fees and taxes reduce what you can spend.** Withdrawals from traditional
  401(k)s and IRAs are generally taxed as income.
- **Real spending isn't rigid.** Most retirees adjust — spending less after a
  bad year, or more on travel early on. Flexible spending can make a plan much
  more resilient.

Because of these uncertainties, many planners test more conservative rates
like 3% or 3.5%, especially for early retirees.

## A Worked Example

Consider someone earning **$80,000** a year, planning to retire in about 30
years. All figures are in today's dollars.

**Step 1: Estimate spending.** Using a 75% replacement ratio:

$80,000 × 0.75 = **$60,000 a year**

**Step 2: Subtract guaranteed income.** Suppose their my Social Security
estimate shows about $2,000 a month at their planned claiming age — $24,000 a
year. (This is an illustrative figure; your own estimate could be higher or
lower.)

$60,000 − $24,000 = **$36,000 a year** that savings must provide

**Step 3: Apply a withdrawal rate.**

| Withdrawal rate | Savings target |
| --- | --- |
| 4% (× 25) | $900,000 |
| 3.5% | $1,028,571 |
| 3% | $1,200,000 |

At 4%, they'd withdraw $36,000 in year one. If inflation runs at 3% that
year, the next year's withdrawal becomes $37,080, and so on — the withdrawal
keeps its purchasing power regardless of how the portfolio performs.

**Step 4: Work out the monthly savings.** To reach $900,000 in today's
dollars, they need to save the following amounts each month at an assumed 6%
annual return (expressed in real terms, after inflation):

| Years until retirement | Monthly savings needed |
| --- | --- |
| 40 | ≈ $452 |
| 30 | ≈ $896 |
| 20 | ≈ $1,948 |

Starting 10 years earlier cuts the required monthly amount roughly in half;
starting 10 years later more than doubles it. Time is the most powerful
variable in the plan.

## Why Today's Dollars Matter

The example above keeps everything in today's dollars, which is the easiest
way to think about it. But your actual account balance will need to be much
larger in future dollars. At 3% inflation, $60,000 of spending today becomes
about **$125,627 a year** in 25 years. A $900,000 target in today's dollars
corresponds to a far larger number on your future statement.

That's not a reason to panic — your income and investments will also grow in
nominal terms — but it is a reason to be consistent. Either use **real**
(after-inflation) return assumptions with today's-dollar targets, or nominal
returns with inflated future targets. Mixing the two makes a plan look far
rosier than it is.

## Other Factors That Change the Number

- **Retirement age.** Retiring earlier means fewer years of saving, more
  years of withdrawals, and possibly a gap before Social Security and
  Medicare. Working a few extra years has a triple effect: more contributions,
  more growth, and fewer years to fund.
- **Health care.** Medicare generally begins at 65, but premiums, deductibles,
  and costs it doesn't cover remain. Retiring before 65 means paying for
  coverage on your own.
- **Longevity.** Planning to age 90 or beyond protects against the risk of
  outliving your money. Pensions and annuities that pay for life can help
  manage that risk.
- **Taxes.** Money in traditional 401(k)s and IRAs is taxed when withdrawn;
  qualified Roth withdrawals are not. The mix of account types affects how
  much you can actually spend.
- **Big one-time costs.** A new roof, a car, or helping a child can require
  larger withdrawals in some years.

## Using Your Employer Plan and IRAs

Tax-advantaged accounts make reaching the target more achievable. A 401(k) or
403(b) lets you save directly from your paycheck, and many employers match
part of your contribution — free money that's usually worth capturing first.
IRAs add another layer of tax-advantaged savings. Contribution limits change
periodically, so check current limits each year.

## Putting It Together

To estimate how much you need to retire, start with your expected annual
spending, subtract Social Security and other guaranteed income, and multiply
the remaining gap by about 25 — the 4% rule — or by more if you want a
larger safety margin or expect a long retirement. Then work backward to the monthly
savings that gets you there, and revisit the estimate every year or two as
your income, spending, and the markets change.
