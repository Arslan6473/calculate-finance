An emergency fund is money set aside for the expenses you can't predict: a
job loss, a car repair, a trip to the emergency room, a furnace that quits in
January. It isn't an investment and it isn't a vacation fund. Its only job is
to be there, in cash, when something goes wrong — so that a bad month doesn't
turn into high-interest debt that lingers for years. This guide explains how
to size an emergency fund for your situation, how to build one without
stalling your other goals, and where to keep it.

## Why an Emergency Fund Matters

Without savings, an unexpected bill has to go somewhere, and that somewhere is
usually a credit card. The Federal Reserve's annual Survey of Household
Economics and Decisionmaking asks adults how they would handle a hypothetical
$400 emergency expense. In the survey conducted in October 2025, 63 percent
said they would cover it entirely with cash, savings, or a credit card paid off
at the next statement — which means more than a third of adults would need to
borrow, sell something, or couldn't cover it at all.

To see what that costs, imagine a $1,500 car repair charged to a card with a
24% APR, paid down at $100 a month. It takes **19 months** to clear and costs
about **$301 in interest** — a 20% surcharge on a repair you had no choice
about. An emergency fund turns that same repair into a withdrawal you
replenish over the following months, with no interest at all.

There's a second, less obvious benefit: options. With several months of
expenses in the bank, a layoff gives you time to find the right job instead of
the first one. A fund lets you say no to a bad deal, leave an unsafe living
situation, or handle a family crisis without making a money decision under
pressure.

## The Common Rule: Three to Six Months of Expenses

The most widely repeated guideline is to save **three to six months of
essential expenses**. Two words in that rule do a lot of work.

**Expenses, not income.** You're sizing the fund to cover what you'd actually
need to spend if your paycheck stopped, not to replace your salary. That
number is usually well below your take-home pay, because some spending —
retirement contributions, dining out, travel — would pause in a real
emergency.

**Essential.** Count the bills that keep a roof over your head and keep you
employable: housing, utilities, food, transportation, insurance, minimum debt
payments, and basic phone and internet. Leave out streaming services,
restaurant meals, and anything you'd cut the first week.

## Calculating Your Number

Start by listing your essential monthly costs. Here's an example for a
single renter:

| Essential expense | Monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | $1,500 |
| Utilities | $200 |
| Groceries | $500 |
| Transportation (car payment, gas, insurance) | $350 |
| Health insurance and out-of-pocket costs | $250 |
| Minimum debt payments | $200 |
| Phone and internet | $100 |
| **Total** | **$3,100** |

From there, the targets are straightforward:

- **Three months:** 3 × $3,100 = **$9,300**
- **Six months:** 6 × $3,100 = **$18,600**

If you don't know your spending, look at the last three months of bank and
credit card statements and average them. Most people underestimate irregular
costs like car registration or annual insurance premiums, so it's worth adding
a monthly allowance for those as well.

## Where You Should Land in the Range

Three months is a reasonable floor for many households; six months or more
makes sense when your risk of a long income gap is higher. Factors that point
toward **the higher end**:

- **One income supports the household.** If that paycheck stops, nothing else
  covers the bills.
- **Variable or commission-based pay.** Freelancers, gig workers, and
  salespeople face uneven months even without an emergency.
- **Self-employment.** There's no unemployment insurance in most cases, and
  slow periods can last.
- **Specialized or senior roles.** Jobs that are harder to replace often take
  longer to find.
- **Dependents, health conditions, or an older home or car.** More people and
  more aging equipment mean more things that can go wrong.

Factors that can justify **the lower end**:

- **Two stable incomes**, where either one could cover essentials alone.
- **In-demand skills** in a field where job searches tend to be short.
- **Other accessible resources**, such as a large taxable brokerage account —
  though investments can fall in value at exactly the wrong time, so they're a
  backup, not a substitute.

Some households in especially uncertain situations aim for nine to twelve
months. There's no single right answer; the goal is to match the size of the
fund to the size and likelihood of the shocks you could face.

## How to Build It Without Stalling Everything Else

Saving $9,300 or $18,600 can feel impossible when you're starting from zero.
Breaking the goal into stages makes it manageable.

**Stage 1: A starter fund.** Aim for $1,000 to one month of expenses first.
This alone handles the most common surprises — a repair, a deductible, a
last-minute flight — and stops the cycle of putting every small emergency on
a card.

**Stage 2: Pay down high-interest debt.** Once a starter fund is in place,
many people focus extra money on credit card balances, since card interest
rates are usually far higher than anything a savings account pays.

**Stage 3: Build to your full target.** With high-interest debt under control,
direct savings back toward three to six months of expenses.

At $400 a month, here's how long the full targets take, assuming the money
earns an illustrative 4% annual yield compounded monthly:

| Goal | Months at $400/month |
| --- | --- |
| $9,300 (three months) | 23 months |
| $18,600 (six months) | 44 months |

Without any interest, the three-month goal would take 24 months instead of
23. Interest helps at the margin, but the monthly contribution does nearly all
the work — which is why automation matters more than chasing the highest rate.

Ways to speed things up:

- **Automate a transfer on payday** so saving happens before spending.
- **Direct windfalls** — tax refunds, bonuses, cash gifts — straight to the
  fund.
- **Save raises.** When your pay goes up, route the difference to savings for
  a few months before adjusting your lifestyle.
- **Trim one recurring cost** and redirect the exact amount.

long your own target will take at different monthly amounts, and the [salary
calculator](/calculators/salary-calculator) helps you see your take-home pay
so you can set a realistic contribution.

## Where to Keep Your Emergency Fund

An emergency fund needs to be **safe, liquid, and separate**:

- **Safe.** The balance shouldn't drop when the stock market does. Deposit
  accounts at FDIC-insured banks — checking, savings, money market deposit
  accounts, and CDs — are insured up to at least $250,000 per depositor, per
  ownership category, at each insured bank. Credit unions have similar
  coverage through the NCUA.
- **Liquid.** You should be able to reach the money within a day or two
  without penalties.
- **Separate.** Keeping the fund in a different account from everyday checking
  makes it less tempting to spend and easier to track.

A high-yield savings account at an insured bank checks all three boxes for
most people. Yields change with interest rates, but the difference matters:
on an $18,600 fund, a 0.5% yield earns about $93 a year, while a 4% yield
earns about $744. Some people hold part of a larger fund in short-term CDs
or Treasury bills for a bit more yield, keeping at least one or two months of
expenses instantly accessible.

Stocks and stock funds are generally a poor home for emergency money. Job
losses often coincide with economic downturns, when market values are also
likely to be down — so you could be forced to sell at a loss right when you
need the cash.

## What Counts as an Emergency

Deciding this in advance prevents the fund from slowly draining. A useful
test is whether the expense is **unexpected, necessary, and urgent**:

- **Yes:** Job loss, medical bills, essential car or home repairs, emergency
  travel for a family crisis, a security deposit after an unexpected move.
- **No:** Holiday gifts, annual subscriptions, a planned vacation, a new phone
  because the old one is slow.

Predictable but irregular costs — car registration, annual premiums, holiday
spending — belong in separate "sinking funds" you contribute to monthly. That
keeps the emergency fund reserved for real surprises.

When you do use the fund, don't feel guilty: that's what it's for. Then make
rebuilding it the priority until it's back at your target.

## Common Mistakes

- **Sizing it to income instead of expenses.** This often produces a target
  so large that people give up before starting.
- **Investing it for higher returns.** The extra yield isn't worth the risk
  of a 20% drop the month you lose your job.
- **Never adjusting the target.** A new baby, a mortgage, or a move to a
  higher-cost city changes your essential expenses. Revisit the number once a
  year.
- **Holding far too much.** Beyond about six to twelve months of expenses,
  extra cash may be better directed to retirement accounts or debt payoff,
  where it can do more work.

## Putting It Together

Your emergency fund target is your essential monthly expenses multiplied by
the number of months of protection you need — typically three to six, and
more if your income is less stable. Start with a small starter fund, deal
with high-interest debt, then build steadily toward the full amount with
automatic transfers. Keep the money in an insured, easy-to-reach account, and
use it only for genuine emergencies.
