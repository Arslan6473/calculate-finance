On a $60,000 salary, the widely used 28/36 rule points to a home price
somewhere around $150,000 to $220,000 — but that range depends on the
details. Your existing debts, your down payment, the interest rate you
qualify for, and your local property taxes can each move the answer by tens
of thousands of dollars. This guide works through the 28/36 rule step by
step on a $60,000 income, shows every assumption, and then tests how the
result changes when those assumptions change.

## Start With Gross Monthly Income

Affordability rules are based on **gross income** — what you earn before
taxes and deductions. On a $60,000 salary:

```
$60,000 ÷ 12 = $5,000 a month
```

If you're buying with someone else, add their gross income too, along with
their debts.

## Apply the 28/36 Rule

The 28/36 rule is a long-standing guideline used in mortgage planning:

- **28% for housing.** Your total monthly housing payment — principal,
  interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and any
  HOA dues — should be no more than 28% of gross monthly income.
- **36% for all debt.** Housing plus every other monthly debt payment should
  be no more than 36% of gross monthly income.

On $5,000 a month:

| Limit | Calculation | Monthly maximum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Housing (28%) | $5,000 × 28% | $1,400 |
| Total debt (36%) | $5,000 × 36% | $1,800 |

Your housing budget is the **lower** of two numbers: the 28% limit, or the
36% limit minus your other debt payments.

### Example: A $300 Car Payment

If your only other debt is a $300 car payment, the 36% limit leaves $1,800 −
$300 = $1,500 for housing. The 28% limit is lower at $1,400, so your housing
budget is **$1,400 a month**.

### Example: $550 in Other Debts

If you have a $300 car payment, a $200 student loan payment, and $50 in
credit card minimums, the 36% limit leaves $1,800 − $550 = $1,250. That's now
lower than $1,400, so your housing budget drops to **$1,250 a month**.

Notice that every dollar of other debt above $400 a month reduces your
housing budget dollar for dollar.

## Turn the Payment Into a Home Price

A housing payment isn't just the mortgage. To convert $1,400 a month into a
price, you have to account for everything that payment covers. Here are the
assumptions for this example — **yours will be different**:

- **Mortgage:** 30-year fixed at **7.00%**. This is an example rate; actual
  rates change weekly and depend on your credit score, down payment, and
  loan type.
- **Property taxes:** 1.0% of the home's value per year. Effective rates vary
  widely by state and county, from well under 1% to more than 2%.
- **Homeowners insurance:** $150 a month. Premiums depend heavily on
  location, the home, and coverage.
- **Private mortgage insurance (PMI):** 0.5% of the loan amount per year when
  the down payment is under 20%. Actual PMI rates depend on your credit
  score and down payment.
- **No HOA dues.**

### With 20% Down

With a $1,400 budget and 20% down, the numbers balance at a price of about
**$203,000**:

| Component | Monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| Principal and interest on a $162,450 loan | $1,081 |
| Property taxes | $169 |
| Homeowners insurance | $150 |
| **Total** | **$1,400** |

You'd need about **$40,600** for the down payment, plus closing costs.

### With 10% Down

A smaller down payment means a larger loan and PMI, so the price drops to
about **$174,000**:

| Component | Monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| Principal and interest on a $156,336 loan | $1,040 |
| Property taxes | $145 |
| Homeowners insurance | $150 |
| PMI | $65 |
| **Total** | **$1,400** |

The down payment here is about **$17,400**. Putting less down gets you into a
home with much less cash, but the price you can afford for the same monthly
payment is about $29,000 lower.

### With More Debt

With $550 in other monthly debts and a $1,250 housing budget, the same
assumptions support a price of about **$179,000** with 20% down or about
**$153,000** with 10% down.

## How Interest Rates Change the Answer

The interest rate has a large effect on purchasing power. Holding everything
else constant with a $1,400 housing budget:

| Mortgage rate | Price with 10% down | Price with 20% down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6.00% | $189,000 | $222,000 |
| 6.50% | $181,000 | $212,000 |
| 7.00% | $174,000 | $203,000 |
| 7.50% | $167,000 | $194,000 |
| 8.00% | $160,000 | $186,000 |

Each half-point change in the rate moves the affordable price by roughly
$7,000 to $10,000 at this income. Shopping among several lenders, and
improving your credit score before you apply, can make a real difference.

## How Property Taxes Change the Answer

Property taxes are paid every month through your escrow account, so they
compete directly with the mortgage for the same budget. With 10% down and a
7.00% rate:

| Property tax rate | Affordable price |
| --- | --- |
| 0.5% of value per year | $184,000 |
| 1.0% of value per year | $174,000 |
| 2.0% of value per year | $156,000 |

The same $60,000 salary buys roughly $29,000 more house in a low-tax area
than in a high-tax one. Look up the actual tax bill for any home you're
considering rather than relying on an average. Homeowners insurance works the
same way: in areas where premiums are high, the affordable price falls.

## What a Lender Might Approve

The 28/36 rule is a planning guideline, not a lender's limit, and lenders
often approve more. Freddie Mac's homebuyer guidance suggests spending no
more than about 30% of gross monthly income on the mortgage payment,
including taxes and insurance — $1,500 on this salary, which would support
about $219,000 with 20% down under the same assumptions.

Total debt limits can go much higher. Fannie Mae's guidelines for
conventional loans allow a total DTI of up to 45% for some manually
underwritten loans and up to 50% for loans approved through its automated
system. At 45% of $5,000, minus a $300 car payment, the housing budget would
be $1,950 — enough, under these assumptions, for a home of about $250,000
with 10% down or $292,000 with 20% down.

That doesn't mean you should borrow that much. A higher payment leaves less
for retirement savings, emergencies, and everything else. Remember that the
ratios use gross income: if your take-home pay is around $4,000 a month, a
$1,950 housing payment plus a $300 car payment would consume more than half
of it.

## Costs the Rule Doesn't Cover

Even a payment within 28% can feel tight if you don't plan for:

- **Maintenance and repairs.** Homes need ongoing upkeep, and big items like
  roofs and water heaters eventually need replacement.
- **Utilities**, which are often higher in a house than in an apartment.
- **Closing costs** due at purchase, in addition to the down payment.
- **Rising taxes and insurance**, which can push your escrow payment up over
  time even with a fixed-rate loan.
- **An emergency fund** — ideally kept separate from your down payment
  savings, so buying the home doesn't leave you with nothing in reserve.

## Ways to Afford More

- **Pay down other debts.** Eliminating a $300 car payment won't raise the
  28% cap, but it can free up room when the 36% limit is the binding one.
- **Save a larger down payment**, which shrinks the loan and can eliminate
  PMI.
- **Improve your credit score** before applying to qualify for a better rate.
- **Look at lower-tax areas** or homes without HOA dues.
- **Buy with a co-borrower**, whose income counts toward the ratios.
- **Ask about first-time buyer and down payment assistance programs**, which
  are offered by many state and local housing agencies.

## Putting It Together

On a $60,000 salary, the 28/36 rule caps housing at $1,400 a month, or less if
your other debts top $400 a month. Under the example assumptions — a 7.00%
rate, 1% property taxes, and $150 a month for insurance — that supports a home
of about $174,000 with 10% down or $203,000 with 20% down. Lower rates, lower
taxes, and fewer debts push the number up; higher ones pull it down. Lenders
may approve you for more, but the 28/36 numbers are a sensible place to
start.
