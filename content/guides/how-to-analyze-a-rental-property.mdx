A rental property can look like a great deal when you compare the rent to the
mortgage payment and see money left over. But that comparison leaves out most
of what it actually costs to own a rental — vacancies, repairs, big-ticket
replacements, management, taxes, and insurance — and it says nothing about how
hard your cash is working compared to other investments. This guide walks
through the standard measures investors use to analyze a rental, how they fit
together, and a full worked example.

## Start With Realistic Income

Begin with **gross scheduled rent**: what the property would collect in a year
if it were rented every single month at market rent.

```
Gross scheduled rent = Monthly rent × 12
```

No property is occupied 100% of the time. Tenants move out, units need to be
cleaned and repaired between leases, and finding a new tenant takes time.
Investors account for this with a **vacancy allowance**, typically expressed
as a percentage of gross rent. A figure around 5% to 8% is common for
planning, though the right number depends on the local market and the
property. Subtract it to get **effective gross income** — what you can
realistically expect to collect.

```
Effective gross income = Gross scheduled rent − Vacancy allowance
```

If the property earns other income — laundry machines, parking fees, storage —
add it here.

## Then Count Every Operating Expense

Operating expenses are the ongoing costs of running the property. They do
**not** include your mortgage payment; financing is handled separately. The
usual categories are:

- **Property taxes.** Look up the actual current bill, and be aware that some
  areas reassess when a property sells.
- **Insurance.** A landlord policy, which differs from (and usually costs more
  than) a homeowners policy.
- **Maintenance and repairs.** Leaky faucets, broken appliances, a door that
  sticks. Many investors budget 5% to 10% of rent, more for older properties.
- **Capital expenditures (CapEx).** The big, infrequent replacements: roof,
  HVAC, water heater, flooring, appliances. They don't happen every year, but
  they will happen, so a monthly reserve smooths the cost. A common planning
  figure is another 5% to 10% of rent.
- **Property management.** Professional managers commonly charge a percentage
  of collected rent, often somewhere around 8% to 12%, plus fees for placing
  new tenants. Even if you plan to self-manage, it's worth including — your
  time has value, and you may not want to manage it forever.
- **HOA dues, utilities the owner pays, landscaping, pest control, and
  accounting**, where applicable.

Underestimating expenses is the most common way rental analyses go wrong. A
property whose numbers only work with zero vacancy and no repairs isn't a good
deal; it's a deal that hasn't been tested yet.

## Net Operating Income (NOI)

Subtract operating expenses from effective gross income and you get **net
operating income**:

```
NOI = Effective gross income − Operating expenses
```

NOI is the property's annual profit *before* mortgage payments and income
taxes. Because it ignores financing, it describes the property itself, not the
way any particular buyer chooses to pay for it. That makes it the foundation
for the next metric.

## Cap Rate

The **capitalization rate** compares NOI to the property's price:

```
Cap rate = NOI ÷ Purchase price
```

Cap rate tells you what return the property would produce if you bought it
entirely with cash. It's the standard way to compare properties of different
sizes and prices on equal footing. A higher cap rate means more income per
dollar of price — but often comes with more risk, such as a weaker location,
an older building, or less reliable tenants. A lower cap rate often reflects a
more desirable, more stable property where buyers accept less income for that
stability. There's no universally "good" cap rate; compare it to similar
properties in the same market.

## Cash Flow

Most investors don't buy with all cash. Once you add a mortgage, the payment
on that loan — called **debt service** — comes out of NOI:

```
Annual cash flow = NOI − Annual debt service
```

Cash flow is the money left in your pocket each year after all operating costs
and the mortgage. Positive cash flow means the property pays for itself;
negative cash flow means you're feeding it money every month.

## Cash-on-Cash Return

Cash flow in dollars doesn't tell you whether it's a good return on what you
put in. **Cash-on-cash return** does:

```
Cash-on-cash return = Annual cash flow ÷ Total cash invested
```

Total cash invested includes the down payment, closing costs, and any up-front
repairs or improvements — everything that came out of your pocket to get the
property operating.

Cash-on-cash return lets you compare a rental against other uses of the same
cash. Keep in mind that it only measures the cash the property throws off. It
leaves out loan principal paydown, appreciation, and tax effects such as
depreciation, all of which can add to your total return.

## The 1% Rule: A Quick Screen

The **1% rule** is a fast filter for deciding whether a property deserves a
closer look: monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price.

```
Monthly rent ÷ Purchase price ≥ 1%
```

A $200,000 property would need to rent for at least $2,000 a month to pass.
The rule is crude — it ignores taxes, insurance, condition, and interest rates
— and in many higher-priced markets, very few properties meet it. Treat it as
a screening tool to quickly weed out listings, never as a substitute for a
full analysis.

## A Worked Example

Consider a single-family home listed at **$200,000** that's expected to rent
for **$1,900 a month**.

**Quick screen:** $1,900 ÷ $200,000 = 0.95%. Just under the 1% rule — not an
automatic pass, but close enough to run the full numbers.

### Income

- Gross scheduled rent: $1,900 × 12 = **$22,800**
- Vacancy allowance (5%): $22,800 × 5% = $1,140
- Effective gross income: $22,800 − $1,140 = **$21,660**

### Operating Expenses

| Expense | Annual cost |
| --- | --- |
| Property taxes | $2,400 |
| Insurance | $1,200 |
| Maintenance (5% of gross rent) | $1,140 |
| CapEx reserve (5% of gross rent) | $1,140 |
| Property management (8% of collected rent: $21,660 × 8%) | $1,732.80 |
| **Total operating expenses** | **$7,612.80** |

### NOI and Cap Rate

- NOI: $21,660 − $7,612.80 = **$14,047.20**
- Cap rate: $14,047.20 ÷ $200,000 = **7.02%**

### Financing, Cash Flow, and Cash-on-Cash Return

Suppose you put 25% down and finance the rest with a 30-year fixed mortgage at
7%:

- Down payment: $200,000 × 25% = $50,000
- Loan amount: $150,000
- Closing costs (assumed): $5,000
- Total cash invested: $50,000 + $5,000 = **$55,000**
- Monthly principal and interest: **$997.95**
- Annual debt service: $997.95 × 12 = **$11,975.40**

Then:

- Annual cash flow: $14,047.20 − $11,975.40 = **$2,071.80**, or about $172.65
  a month
- Cash-on-cash return: $2,071.80 ÷ $55,000 = **3.77%**

### What the Example Shows

The property produces a 7.02% cap rate, but the investor's cash-on-cash return
is only about 3.8%. That gap comes from the financing. The annual debt service
is $11,975.40 on a $150,000 loan, or about 7.98% of the loan amount each year
— more than the 7.02% the property earns on its price. When borrowing costs
more than the property yields, leverage *reduces* your cash return rather than
amplifying it. Investors call this negative leverage.

That doesn't automatically make it a bad investment. The owner is also paying
down the loan principal every month and may benefit from appreciation and tax
deductions. But it does mean the deal depends more on those factors and less
on monthly cash flow. A small surprise — a two-month vacancy, a $5,000 roof
repair — could wipe out a full year's cash flow.

Changing the inputs shows what drives the result. With a larger down payment,
debt service drops and cash flow rises; because this deal has negative
leverage, cash-on-cash return also rises toward the 7.02% cap rate, though
more of your money is tied up in one property. A lower interest rate or a
lower purchase price improves cash-on-cash return quickly.

## Common Mistakes

- **Comparing rent to the mortgage payment only.** It ignores vacancy and
  operating expenses, which together come to about 38% of gross rent in the
  example above ($1,140 + $7,612.80 = $8,752.80 of $22,800).
- **Assuming full occupancy.** Even good properties sit empty between tenants.
- **Skipping the CapEx reserve.** Roofs and furnaces wear out on their own
  schedule, not yours.
- **Using the seller's numbers.** Verify rents against comparable listings and
  expenses against actual tax bills and insurance quotes.
- **Leaving out your own time.** Self-management saves the fee but costs hours
  and attention.

## Putting It Together

Start with realistic income after vacancy, subtract every operating expense to
find NOI, and divide by the price to get the cap rate. Then subtract debt
service to find cash flow, and divide by the cash you put in to get
cash-on-cash return. Use the 1% rule to screen listings quickly, but never to
make the final decision.
