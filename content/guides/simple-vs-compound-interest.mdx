Interest is the price of using money. When you borrow, you pay it; when you
save or invest, you earn it. But two loans or accounts with the same stated
rate can produce very different results depending on one question: is
interest calculated only on the original amount, or on a balance that already
includes past interest? That's the difference between **simple** and
**compound** interest, and over long periods it becomes enormous. This guide
explains both, shows the math side by side, and covers where you'll run into
each one.

## Simple Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the **principal** — the original amount
borrowed or deposited. The formula is:

Interest = principal × rate × time

If you deposit $10,000 at 5% simple interest, you earn $10,000 × 5% = **$500
every year**, no matter how long the money stays put. After 10 years you've
earned $5,000 in interest; after 30 years, $15,000. The interest each year is
identical because the base it's calculated on never changes.

Simple interest grows in a straight line. If you plotted the balance on a
chart, it would rise by the same amount every year.

## Compound Interest

Compound interest is calculated on the **current balance**, which includes
interest you've already earned. Each period's interest is added to the
balance, and the next period's interest is calculated on that larger amount.
The formula for a balance compounded once a year is:

Future value = principal × (1 + rate) ^ years

The same $10,000 at 5% compounded annually earns $500 in year one, bringing
the balance to $10,500. In year two, 5% applies to $10,500, earning $525.
Year three earns about $551, and so on. The rate never changes, but the
dollar amount of interest grows every year:

| Year | Interest earned that year |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | $500.00 |
| 10 | $775.66 |
| 20 | $1,263.48 |
| 30 | $2,058.07 |

By year 30, compounding is producing more than four times as much interest
per year as simple interest does on the same deposit.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

Here's $10,000 at 5% over different time horizons:

| Years | Simple interest balance | Compound interest balance | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | $15,000 | $16,289 | $1,289 |
| 20 | $20,000 | $26,533 | $6,533 |
| 30 | $25,000 | $43,219 | $18,219 |

Over 10 years, compounding adds a modest $1,289. Over 30 years, it adds more
than $18,000 — more than the original deposit itself. The gap doesn't grow
steadily; it accelerates, because compounding is exponential while simple
interest is linear.

At higher rates the effect is even stronger. $10,000 at 7% for 30 years grows
to $31,000 with simple interest but about **$76,123** with annual compounding.

## How Compounding Frequency Changes the Result

Compound interest can be added annually, quarterly, monthly, or daily. The
more often it compounds, the sooner each bit of interest starts earning its
own interest. For a balance compounded *n* times per year:

Future value = principal × (1 + rate ÷ n) ^ (n × years)

Here's $10,000 at a 6% stated rate for 10 years:

| Compounding | Balance after 10 years | Effective annual yield |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annually | $17,908 | 6.00% |
| Quarterly | $18,140 | 6.14% |
| Monthly | $18,194 | 6.17% |
| Daily | $18,220 | 6.18% |

Going from annual to monthly compounding adds about $285 over a decade. Going
from monthly to daily adds only about $26 more. Frequency matters, but with
sharply diminishing returns — the rate and the length of time matter far more.

The **effective annual yield** column is why banks advertise savings accounts
using **APY (annual percentage yield)**, which builds in the effect of
compounding. When you compare savings accounts, compare APYs. When you compare
loans, remember that the stated APR doesn't always reflect how often interest
compounds.

## Where You'll See Each Type

**Simple interest (or interest on principal only) shows up in:**

- **Many auto loans and personal installment loans,** where interest accrues
  on the outstanding principal and unpaid interest isn't added to the
  balance. As you pay down principal, the interest portion of each payment
  falls.
- **Mortgages,** which work similarly: each month's interest is based on the
  remaining principal, and a fixed payment covers that interest plus some
  principal.
- **Bonds that pay out their interest,** such as Treasury notes paying a
  fixed coupon every six months. If you spend the coupons instead of
  reinvesting them, your return behaves like simple interest.

**Compound interest shows up in:**

- **Savings accounts, money market accounts, and CDs,** which typically
  compound daily or monthly.
- **Credit cards,** which commonly compound daily on unpaid balances —
  compounding working against you.
- **Investments where earnings are reinvested,** such as mutual funds with
  dividends reinvested. Market returns aren't a fixed interest rate, but
  reinvested growth compounds in the same way.
- **Some student loans,** where unpaid interest can be capitalized — added to
  the principal — after certain events, so you then pay interest on interest.

## Compounding Works Both Ways

Compounding is a powerful ally when you're saving and a costly one when
you're borrowing. A $1,000 credit card balance at a 24% APR, compounded daily
and left untouched for a year, accrues about **$271** in interest — not the
$240 a simple 24% would suggest. Leave it longer and the balance snowballs,
because each month's interest becomes part of the balance that next month's
interest is charged on.

That's why paying off high-rate compounding debt is often one of the best
"returns" available: every dollar you pay down stops compounding against you.

## Adding Regular Contributions

Most people don't make a single deposit and walk away; they save a little
every month. Compounding applies to every contribution from the day it lands.

Suppose you save **$200 a month** at a 6% annual return, compounded monthly:

- **After 30 years:** you've contributed $72,000, and the balance is about
  **$200,903**. Nearly two-thirds of the ending balance is growth, not money
  you put in.
- **After 40 years:** the same $200 a month grows to about **$398,298** —
  almost double the 30-year figure from just ten more years of saving.

This is the practical case for starting early. Someone who starts saving $200
a month at 25 and stops at 65 ends up with roughly twice as much as someone
who starts at 35, even though they contributed only $24,000 more.

## The Rule of 72

A quick way to estimate compounding's effect is the **Rule of 72**: divide 72
by the annual interest rate to approximate how many years it takes for money
to double. At 6%, money doubles in about 72 ÷ 6 = **12 years** (the exact
answer is about 11.9). At 9%, it doubles in about 8 years.

The rule only applies to compound interest. With simple interest at 6%, it
takes a full 100 ÷ 6 ≈ 16.7 years to double, and it never accelerates after
that.

## How to Tell Which One You're Getting

The paperwork usually tells you, if you know where to look:

- **Savings accounts and CDs** must disclose an APY. If the APY is higher
  than the stated interest rate, interest compounds; the gap shows how much
  compounding adds.
- **Loan agreements** describe how interest accrues. Look for phrases like
  "interest accrues daily on the unpaid principal balance" (interest on
  principal only) or "unpaid interest will be capitalized" (interest on
  interest).
- **Credit card agreements** explain the balance calculation method and
  whether interest is compounded daily.
- **Investment statements** show whether dividends and distributions are
  reinvested or paid out as cash.

When in doubt, ask the lender or bank directly how often interest is
calculated and whether it is ever added to the balance.

## Putting It Together

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal and grows in a
straight line. Compound interest is calculated on a growing balance and
accelerates over time, with the effect becoming dramatic over decades. More
frequent compounding helps slightly, but the rate and — above all — time do
most of the work. When you save, compounding is your best friend; when you
carry high-rate debt, it's working just as hard against you.
