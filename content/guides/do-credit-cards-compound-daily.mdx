Your credit card statement shows interest once a month, so it's easy to
assume it's calculated once a month. On most cards it isn't. Interest is
usually calculated every day, and on most cards each day's interest is added
to the balance — so tomorrow's interest is charged on today's interest. This
guide explains how daily compounding works on a credit card, how much it
actually adds, how to check your own card's method, and how to keep it from
costing you anything.

## The Short Answer

**Yes — most credit cards compound interest daily.** The issuer converts your
APR into a daily rate, applies it to your balance each day, and adds the
result to the balance. The total is posted once per statement, which is why
it looks monthly.

Daily compounding only matters if you carry a balance. If you pay your full
statement balance by the due date every month, the grace period means new
purchases are charged no interest at all, so there's nothing to compound.

## How Daily Compounding Works

### Step 1: Convert the APR to a Daily Rate

Issuers divide the APR by 365 (some use 360) to get the **daily periodic
rate**:

```
Daily rate = APR ÷ 365
```

At a 24% APR: 0.24 ÷ 365 = **0.06575% per day**.

### Step 2: Apply It Every Day

Each day, the issuer multiplies that day's balance by the daily rate. With
daily compounding, the interest is added to the balance immediately, so the
next day's calculation starts from a slightly larger number:

```
Balance after d days = Starting balance × (1 + APR ÷ 365)^d
```

### A One-Month Example

Take a **$3,000** balance at **24% APR** over a 30-day billing cycle, with no
new purchases or payments:

- **Without compounding:** $3,000 × 0.0006575 × 30 = **$59.18**
- **With daily compounding:** $3,000 × [(1.0006575)^30 − 1] = **$59.75**

Daily compounding adds **57 cents** this month. On its own, that's small.

## How Much It Adds Over a Year

The effect grows over longer periods, because the interest keeps compounding
on itself. On a **$1,000** balance at **24% APR** left untouched for a year:

| Method | Interest for the year | Effective annual rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No compounding (APR × balance) | $240.00 | 24.00% |
| Compounded monthly | $268.24 | 26.82% |
| Compounded daily | $271.15 | 27.11% |

The **effective annual rate** — the rate after compounding — is the real
annual cost of the balance. Credit card APRs don't include compounding, so
the effective rate is always higher than the APR when you carry a balance:

| APR | Effective annual rate (daily compounding) |
| --- | --- |
| 18% | 19.72% |
| 24% | 27.11% |
| 30% | 34.97% |

![Column chart comparing APR with the effective annual rate under daily compounding: 18% becomes 19.72%, 24% becomes 27.11%, and 30% becomes 34.97%](/images/guides/do-credit-cards-compound-daily-effective-rate.svg "Daily compounding pushes the true annual cost above the stated APR.")

Most of the difference between the APR and the effective rate comes from
compounding at all, not from compounding daily instead of monthly. Daily
compounding costs only about 0.3 percentage points more than monthly at a
24% APR. The bigger issue is the high APR itself.

## How to Tell If Your Card Compounds Daily

Your card agreement explains it, usually in a section called something like
"How We Calculate Your Balance" or "How Interest Is Calculated," just below
the rate and fee table. Look for language like:

- **"Daily balance method (including new transactions)"** — interest is
  calculated on each day's balance.
- **"We add interest to your balance daily"** or "**interest charges are
  added to the daily balance**" — that's daily compounding.
- **"Average daily balance (including new transactions)"** — interest is
  charged on the average of each day's balance. Many agreements using this
  method also add each day's interest to the next day's balance, which
  compounds it.

If the agreement isn't clear, call the issuer and ask: "Is interest added to
my balance daily, and is it included in the balance that's charged interest
the next day?"

## Daily Compounding vs. Average Daily Balance

These are two different ideas that often appear together:

- **Average daily balance** is about *which balance* gets charged interest:
  the average of every day's balance in the cycle, rather than the balance
  on a single day.
- **Daily compounding** is about *whether interest earns interest*: whether
  each day's interest charge becomes part of the balance.

A card can use one, both, or neither. Most major cards use an average daily
or daily balance method and compound daily. For a step-by-step look at the
average daily balance calculation, see [how credit card interest
works](/guides/how-credit-card-interest-works).

## Why Daily Compounding Makes Payment Timing Matter

Because interest accrues every day, *when* you pay matters, not just how
much:

- **Paying earlier in the cycle** lowers the balance for more days, which
  lowers the interest.
- **Splitting a payment in two** — half mid-cycle and half by the due date —
  usually costs less interest than one payment on the due date.
- **Large purchases** start accruing interest the day they post if you're
  already carrying a balance.

On a $3,000 balance at 24%, paying $1,000 on day 1 of a 30-day cycle instead
of day 30 saves about $19 in interest that month.

## How to Avoid Compounding Interest Entirely

- **Pay the full statement balance by the due date.** The grace period
  covers new purchases, so no interest is charged and nothing compounds. See
  [when credit card interest starts](/guides/when-does-credit-card-interest-start)
  for how the grace period works.
- **If you carry a balance, pay more than the minimum** and pay as early as
  you can.
- **Avoid cash advances,** which typically start accruing interest
  immediately with no grace period.
- **Consider a 0% balance transfer** to stop interest while you pay down
  debt, after checking the transfer fee and the rate when the promotion ends.

## Putting It Together

Most credit cards calculate interest daily and add it to your balance, so
interest compounds. At a 24% APR, that makes the true annual cost about 27%.
The extra cost from compounding is modest compared with the APR itself, and
it disappears completely if you pay your statement in full each month.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do all credit cards compound daily?** Most do, but not all. The method is
spelled out in your card agreement. Some cards compound monthly, and the
difference in cost between daily and monthly compounding is small.

**Is credit card interest charged daily or monthly?** It's usually
calculated daily and billed monthly. Each statement shows the total interest
for the billing cycle.

**Does daily compounding mean I pay interest every day?** Interest accrues
every day you carry a balance past the grace period, but you pay it once a
month as part of your statement.

**How do I calculate daily credit card interest?** Divide the APR by 365 and
multiply by your balance. At 24% APR, a $3,000 balance accrues about $1.97
of interest per day.
