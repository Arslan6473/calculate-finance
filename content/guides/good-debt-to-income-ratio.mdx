Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is one of the first numbers a lender checks,
but there's no single cutoff that applies everywhere. Conventional, FHA, VA,
and USDA mortgages each set their own limits, auto and personal lenders use
their own, and the maximum a lender will approve is usually well above what's
comfortable to live with. This guide lays out what counts as a good DTI in
general and the limits for each major loan type.

## What Is a Good Debt-to-Income Ratio?

DTI is your total monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly
income:

```
DTI = Monthly debt payments ÷ Gross monthly income × 100
```

As a general guide for the **back-end ratio** — all debt payments, including
housing:

| DTI | What it generally means |
| --- | --- |
| Under 36% | **Good.** Within the traditional 28/36 guideline; most loans are available and the budget has breathing room. |
| 36% to 43% | **Manageable.** Most mortgage programs still approve it, but expect closer scrutiny and less flexibility in the budget. |
| 43% to 50% | **High.** Some programs allow it with strong credit, savings, or automated approval; the budget is tight. |
| Over 50% | **Very high.** Few mortgage options; more than half of pre-tax income already goes to debt. |

For the **front-end ratio** — housing alone — 28% or less is the traditional
target.

If you haven't calculated yours yet, start with [how to calculate your
debt-to-income ratio](/guides/how-to-calculate-debt-to-income-ratio).

## DTI Limits by Loan Type

Limits depend on the program, how the loan is underwritten, and your credit
score and savings. Individual lenders can also set stricter limits than the
program allows.

| Loan type | Typical housing (front-end) limit | Typical total (back-end) limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional, manual underwriting | — | 36%, up to 45% with strong credit and reserves |
| Conventional, automated underwriting (Fannie Mae) | — | Up to 50% |
| FHA | 31% | 43%, higher with compensating factors or automated approval |
| VA | — | 41% guideline, combined with residual income |
| USDA | 29% | 41% |
| Jumbo | Varies by lender | Often around 43%, sometimes lower |

![Bar chart of typical maximum back-end DTI by loan type: conventional manual 36% (45% with factors), conventional automated 50%, FHA 43%, VA 41%, USDA 41%](/images/guides/good-debt-to-income-ratio-limits.svg "Typical back-end DTI limits; approvals above these depend on compensating factors.")

### Conventional Loans

Conventional loans that follow Fannie Mae's guidelines set the limits most
lenders use:

- **Manually underwritten loans:** a maximum total DTI of **36%**, which can
  rise to **45%** if the borrower meets higher credit score and cash reserve
  requirements.
- **Loans approved through Fannie Mae's automated system (Desktop
  Underwriter):** a maximum of **50%**.

Most conventional loans today are approved through automated underwriting,
which is why you'll often hear "50%" as the conventional maximum. In
practice, a ratio near 50% is approved only when the rest of the application
is strong.

### FHA Loans

FHA loans are designed for borrowers with smaller down payments or lower
credit scores. Under HUD's underwriting guidelines, manually underwritten FHA
loans use benchmark ratios of **31% housing and 43% total**. Those limits can
go higher when a borrower has **compensating factors**, such as significant
cash reserves, a minimal increase in housing payment, or residual income.
Loans approved through FHA's automated scorecard can be approved at higher
ratios when the rest of the file supports it.

### VA Loans

VA loans use a **41%** DTI guideline, but it isn't a hard cap. VA also
requires **residual income** — the money left each month after major
expenses, which varies by family size and region. A borrower over 41% can
still be approved with a written justification from the lender, and if
residual income is at least 20% above VA's guideline, that extra review isn't
required. Conversely, a borrower under 41% still needs adequate residual
income.

### USDA Loans

USDA guaranteed loans for rural and some suburban areas set a **29%** limit
for the housing payment (principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and
association dues) and a **41%** limit for total debt. Loans can exceed these
with compensating factors.

### Jumbo Loans

Jumbo loans exceed the conforming loan limits that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac
can buy, so each lender sets its own rules. Many cap DTI around 43%, and
some go lower or require larger reserves in exchange for a higher ratio.

## Auto Loans and Personal Loans

Car lenders and personal loan lenders don't publish universal DTI limits.
Many prefer a total DTI under about 40% to 50%, including the new payment,
and weigh it alongside your credit score and income stability. A lower DTI
often qualifies you for a better rate, not just an approval.

## A Worked Example by Loan Type

Say your gross income is **$7,000** a month, and you have **$600** a month
in car and student loan payments. You're considering a home with a total
housing payment of **$2,100**.

- **Front-end DTI:** $2,100 ÷ $7,000 = **30.0%**
- **Back-end DTI:** ($2,100 + $600) ÷ $7,000 = $2,700 ÷ $7,000 = **38.6%**

How that plays out:

| Program | Housing limit | Total limit | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional (automated) | — | 50% | Within the limit |
| Conventional (manual) | — | 36% (45% with factors) | Needs strong credit and reserves |
| FHA | 31% | 43% | Within both benchmarks |
| VA | — | 41% | Within the guideline; residual income must also qualify |
| USDA | 29% | 41% | Housing ratio slightly over 29% |

The same borrower looks different under each program. To bring the USDA
housing ratio down to 29%, the housing payment would need to fall to
$2,030 a month.

## The Maximum vs. What's Comfortable

Being approved at 45% or 50% doesn't mean the payment is affordable. DTI
ignores taxes, groceries, utilities, child care, and saving for retirement. At
a 50% DTI, half of your pre-tax income goes to debt payments before any of
those are paid.

A few signs your DTI is high for your budget, even if a lender approves it:

- You can't save at least 10% to 15% of income for retirement.
- You don't have an emergency fund, or can't add to it.
- Unexpected costs go on a credit card.

## How to Improve Your DTI

- **Pay off small debts entirely.** A paid-off card or loan removes its
  payment from the ratio completely.
- **Target the largest payment relative to its balance,** often a car loan
  near the end of its term.
- **Avoid new debt before applying,** including financing furniture or a new
  car.
- **Add documented income,** such as a co-borrower or stable second job.
- **Lower the housing payment** with a cheaper home, a larger down payment,
  or by shopping for insurance.

## Putting It Together

A DTI under 36% is good by almost any standard. Between 36% and 43%, most
mortgage programs still work. Above 43%, you'll depend on automated
approval, compensating factors, or a program with higher limits — and the
budget will be tight. Know which program you're applying for, because
conventional, FHA, VA, and USDA loans each measure the ratio a little
differently.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is 40% a good debt-to-income ratio?** It's above the traditional 36%
guideline but within the limits of most mortgage programs. Lenders may look
more closely at your credit and savings, and your monthly budget will have
less room.

**What is the maximum DTI for a mortgage?** For conventional loans through
Fannie Mae's automated system, 50%. FHA and VA loans can be approved above
their benchmarks with compensating factors. There's no universal maximum.

**Does DTI include rent?** When you apply for a mortgage, the lender uses the
proposed mortgage payment instead of your current rent. For other loans,
lenders often include rent in your monthly obligations.

**Does DTI affect my credit score?** No. Credit reports don't include income,
so DTI isn't part of your credit score. Lenders calculate it separately.
