Compound interest is often summarized as "interest on interest," which is
accurate but doesn't quite convey why it matters so much. The real story is
about acceleration: growth that starts slow and keeps speeding up, for as
long as the money stays invested. This guide walks through the formula, shows
how the growth unfolds year by year, and covers the factors that help or hurt
compounding in real life — rate, time, fees, inflation, and regular
contributions.

## Simple Interest vs. Compound Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount you deposited. If
you put $1,000 in an account earning 5% simple interest, you'd earn $50 every
year, forever — a flat, unchanging amount. Compound interest is calculated on
your current balance, which includes any interest already added. That same
$1,000 at 5% compounded annually earns $50 in year one, bringing the balance
to $1,050. In year two, the 5% applies to $1,050, not $1,000 — earning $52.50
instead. The gap seems tiny at first, but it widens every year.

For a deeper side-by-side comparison of the two methods, including where
you'll encounter each one, see [simple vs. compound
interest](/guides/simple-vs-compound-interest).

## The Compound Interest Formula

The balance after compounding is:

```
Future value = P × (1 + r ÷ n) ^ (n × t)
```

- **P** is the starting principal.
- **r** is the stated annual rate, as a decimal (7% = 0.07).
- **n** is the number of times interest compounds per year (1 for annually,
  12 for monthly, 365 for daily).
- **t** is the number of years.

With annual compounding, n is 1 and the formula simplifies to P × (1 + r) ^ t.
The exponent is what makes compounding powerful: time isn't multiplied into
the result, it's raised as a power.

## Why the Effect Accelerates Over Time

Because each period's interest is added to a slightly larger base than the
one before, the dollar amount of interest earned grows every period too — even
though the rate never changes. Over a few years the difference from simple
interest is minor. Over a few decades, it's dramatic. $10,000 at 7% simple
interest over 30 years grows to $31,000. The same $10,000 at 7% compounded
annually grows to about $76,000 — more than double, purely from interest
building on interest.

Here's what that $10,000 at 7% looks like along the way:

| Year | Interest earned that year | Balance at year end |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $700.00 | $10,700.00 |
| 2 | $749.00 | $11,449.00 |
| 5 | $917.56 | $14,025.52 |
| 10 | $1,286.92 | $19,671.51 |
| 20 | $2,531.57 | $38,696.84 |
| 30 | $4,979.98 | $76,122.55 |

Two things stand out. First, the interest earned in year 30 is about seven
times the interest earned in year 1, at exactly the same rate. Second, the
balance grows by about $37,426 in the final 10 years alone — more than the
$28,697 it grew in the first 20 years combined. Compounding back-loads its
rewards, which is why cutting the last decade off a plan costs far more than
cutting off the first one.

## The Rate Matters More Than It Looks

Small differences in the annual rate turn into large differences over long
periods, because the rate is compounded on itself every year. Here's $10,000
left alone for 30 years at different annual rates (examples, not forecasts):

| Annual rate | Balance after 30 years |
| --- | --- |
| 4% | $32,434 |
| 6% | $57,435 |
| 8% | $100,627 |
| 10% | $174,494 |

Doubling the rate from 4% to 8% more than triples the ending balance. That
asymmetry cuts both ways — it's also why fees and inflation, covered below,
do more damage than their size suggests.

## Compounding Frequency Matters, But Less Than Time Does

Interest can compound annually, monthly, daily, or on almost any schedule a
financial product defines. More frequent compounding produces a slightly
higher return at the same stated annual rate, because interest starts earning
its own interest sooner. The effect is real, but it's modest compared to the
two variables that matter far more: the rate itself, and — above all — how
long the money stays invested.

You can see the effect in the **annual percentage yield (APY)**, which savings
accounts disclose so you can compare them on equal terms. APY converts a
stated rate and compounding schedule into the equivalent yield for one year:

```
APY = (1 + r ÷ n) ^ n − 1
```

A savings account paying a 4.5% stated rate (an example rate) works out to:

- **Compounded monthly:** an APY of about 4.59%.
- **Compounded daily:** an APY of about 4.60%.

The difference between monthly and daily compounding here is less than a
hundredth of a percentage point. When comparing accounts, compare APYs
directly and don't pay much attention to the compounding schedule itself.

## Starting Early: A Worked Example

Consider two savers, both earning an assumed 7% a year, compounded annually,
with each deposit made at the end of the year:

- **Saver A** invests $5,000 a year from age 25 to 35 — 10 deposits, $50,000
  in total — then stops contributing and leaves the money alone.
- **Saver B** waits until 35, then invests $5,000 a year until 65 — 30
  deposits, $150,000 in total.

| | Saver A | Saver B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Years contributing | 10 | 30 |
| Total contributed | $50,000 | $150,000 |
| Balance at 35 | $69,082 | $0 |
| Balance at 65 | $525,872 | $472,304 |

Saver A put in one-third as much money and still finishes ahead, because
those early dollars had 30 extra years to compound. In practice the best
outcome is to start early *and* keep going — but the example shows how much
time is worth compared with the size of the deposits.

Regular contributions compound too. Investing $300 a month at an assumed 7%
annual rate, compounded monthly, grows to about $365,991 after 30 years, from
$108,000 of contributions. The [savings goal
calculator](/calculators/savings-goal-calculator) works this problem in
reverse, telling you what to save each month to hit a target.

## What Works Against Compounding

The headline rate is rarely what you actually keep. Three things quietly
lower the effective rate that compounds:

- **Fees.** An investment that earns 7% before costs but charges 1% a year
  effectively compounds at about 6%. Over 30 years, $10,000 grows to about
  $57,435 instead of $76,123 — a difference of about $18,688, almost twice
  the original deposit. Fees compound against you just as returns compound
  for you.
- **Inflation.** A 7% nominal return with 3% annual inflation is a real
  return of about 3.9% (1.07 ÷ 1.03 − 1). In today's purchasing power, the
  $76,123 from the earlier example is worth only about $31,361. The
  [inflation calculator](/calculators/inflation-calculator) and the guide to
  [how inflation affects savings](/guides/how-inflation-affects-savings) go
  further into this.
- **Taxes and withdrawals.** Money taken out — whether to pay taxes on
  earnings each year or to spend — stops compounding. Tax-advantaged
  retirement accounts are valuable partly because they let more of the
  growth stay invested.

## Common Misconceptions

**"Compounding means my investments grow by a fixed percentage."** Savings
accounts and CDs pay a stated rate, but stocks and funds don't. Their returns
vary year to year, and a few bad years can set the balance back. Long-run
projections use an assumed average rate as a planning tool, not a promise.

**"I've missed my chance, so there's no point starting."** Compounding still
works on every dollar from the day it's invested. Starting later means you
need to contribute more to reach the same goal, not that the effort is
wasted.

**"Compounding only helps savers."** Credit cards and some loans compound
too. A high-rate balance left unpaid grows the same way an investment does —
just against you.

## Solving for Time or Rate

The formula can be rearranged to answer practical questions:

```
Years needed = ln(target ÷ starting amount) ÷ ln(1 + r)
Rate needed  = (target ÷ starting amount) ^ (1 ÷ years) − 1
```

At an assumed 7% a year, $10,000 takes about 23.8 years to grow to $50,000
and about 10.2 years to double. To turn $10,000 into $25,000 in 15 years, you
need an annual return of about 6.3%. The [CAGR
calculator](/calculators/cagr-calculator) does the second calculation for any
start value, end value, and time period.

## Putting It Together

Compound interest rewards time more than almost anything else. Starting
early, even with a small amount, often outperforms starting later with a
much larger sum, simply because the earlier money has had more compounding
periods to work through. Beyond time, the levers are the rate you actually
keep after fees and inflation and how much you keep adding; compounding
frequency is a minor fourth.
