Rate of return answers a simple question: how much did your money grow,
relative to what you put in? The basic formula takes seconds. The harder
part is choosing the right version — whether to include dividends, how to
compare investments held for different lengths of time, and what to do when
you added or withdrew money along the way. This guide walks through each
method with worked examples so you can calculate a return you can actually
compare.

## The Basic Rate of Return Formula

The simple rate of return compares what an investment is worth now (or what
you sold it for) to what you paid:

```
Rate of return = (Ending value − Starting value + Income) ÷ Starting value × 100
```

"Income" is any cash the investment paid you while you held it —
dividends, interest, or rent. Leaving it out understates your return.

### Worked Example

You buy 100 shares at $50, for **$5,000**. Over three years the shares pay
**$300** in dividends, and you sell them at $62, for **$6,200**.

```
Rate of return = ($6,200 − $5,000 + $300) ÷ $5,000 = $1,500 ÷ $5,000 = 30%
```

Your **total return** is 30%. Without the dividends, it would have looked
like 24%.

If you paid trading fees, subtract them from the gain (or add them to your
cost). A $20 fee on the purchase and another on the sale would lower the
gain to $1,460 and the return to 29.2%.

## Annualized Rate of Return

A 30% total return is excellent over one year and mediocre over ten. To
compare investments held for different periods, convert the total return to
an **annualized return** — the steady yearly rate that would produce the
same result with compounding:

```
Annualized return = (1 + total return)^(1 ÷ years) − 1
```

For the example above, held for three years:

```
(1 + 0.30)^(1 ÷ 3) − 1 = 1.0914 − 1 = 9.14% per year
```

This is the same calculation as the **compound annual growth rate (CAGR)**.
Notice it's lower than 30% ÷ 3 = 10%. Simply dividing by the number of years
ignores compounding and overstates the yearly return.

Here's how much the holding period changes the picture for the same 30%
total return:

| Holding period | Total return | Annualized return |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 year | 30% | 30.00% |
| 3 years | 30% | 9.14% |
| 5 years | 30% | 5.39% |
| 10 years | 30% | 2.66% |

![Bar chart of a 30% total return annualized over different holding periods: 30% over 1 year, 9.14% over 3 years, 5.39% over 5 years, and 2.66% over 10 years](/images/guides/how-to-calculate-rate-of-return-annualized.svg "The same total return means very different things over different time frames.")

### For Periods That Aren't Whole Years

Use the exact time in years. An investment held 18 months uses 1.5; one held
200 days uses 200 ÷ 365. Be cautious annualizing very short periods — a 5%
gain in one month annualizes to almost 80%, which says little about what to
expect going forward.

## Rate of Return When You Add or Withdraw Money

The formulas above assume you invested once and didn't touch the account.
Most real portfolios have deposits and withdrawals, and those distort the
simple formula: a deposit makes the balance rise without any investment
gain.

There are two standard ways to handle this.

### Money-Weighted Return (IRR)

The **money-weighted return**, or internal rate of return (IRR), finds the
single annual rate that makes all your deposits and withdrawals grow to the
ending value. It reflects your actual experience, including the timing of
your contributions.

Say you invest **$10,000** at the start, add **$5,000** after one year, and
the account is worth **$17,600** at the end of year two. The IRR is the rate
*r* that solves:

```
$10,000 × (1 + r)² + $5,000 × (1 + r) = $17,600
```

The answer is **r = 10%** per year. You can find it by trial and error or
with the spreadsheet function `=IRR()` for evenly spaced cash flows, or
`=XIRR()` when the dates are irregular.

### Time-Weighted Return

The **time-weighted return** removes the effect of deposits and
withdrawals, so it measures how the investments themselves performed. You
calculate the return for each period between cash flows and chain them
together.

Continuing the example, suppose the account fell to **$9,000** by the end of
year one, just before the $5,000 deposit, bringing it to $14,000:

- **Year 1:** $9,000 ÷ $10,000 − 1 = **−10%**
- **Year 2:** $17,600 ÷ $14,000 − 1 = **+25.71%**
- **Combined:** (1 − 0.10) × (1 + 0.2571) − 1 = **13.14%** over two years, or
  **6.37%** a year

Why the gap between 10% and 6.37%? You added money right before the strong
year, so more of your dollars earned the 25.71%. The money-weighted return
rewards that timing; the time-weighted return ignores it. Fund managers
report time-weighted returns because they don't control when investors add
money. For judging your own results, the money-weighted return is usually
the more relevant number.

## Real Rate of Return

A return in dollars isn't the same as a gain in purchasing power. The
**real rate of return** adjusts for inflation:

```
Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation rate) − 1
```

The 9.14% annualized return from the first example, with 3% inflation, is a
real return of 1.0914 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = **5.96%** a year. Subtracting taxes, if
the investment is in a taxable account, lowers it further.

## Rate of Return vs. ROI

The terms overlap. **ROI** (return on investment) is the same idea as the
simple rate of return and is used for everything from stock trades to
marketing campaigns and rental properties. "Rate of return" usually
implies a percentage over a period, and in investing it's common to quote
it annualized. Whatever you call it, say which version you mean — total or
annualized, with or without income, before or after fees and inflation.

## Common Mistakes

- **Ignoring dividends and interest.** Price change alone isn't your full
  return.
- **Dividing the total return by the number of years.** Use the annualized
  formula instead.
- **Counting deposits as gains.** If you added money, use IRR or a
  time-weighted return.
- **Forgetting fees.** Expense ratios, advisory fees, and trading costs all
  reduce what you actually earned.
- **Comparing nominal and real returns.** Make sure both numbers are
  measured the same way before comparing them.

## Putting It Together

For a single investment you bought and sold, add income to the price change
and divide by what you paid. To compare investments held for different
lengths of time, annualize the result. If you added or withdrew money,
use the money-weighted return (IRR) for your own results or the
time-weighted return to judge the investment itself. Then adjust for
inflation to see your real gain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a good rate of return on an investment?** It depends on the risk.
Insured savings typically return a few percent at most. Diversified stock
portfolios have historically returned more over long periods, with large
swings from year to year. Compare an investment to alternatives with similar
risk.

**How do I calculate rate of return in Excel?** For a simple return,
`=(B2-A2+C2)/A2` with the starting value in A2, ending value in B2, and
income in C2. To annualize, `=(1+D2)^(1/E2)-1` with the total return in D2
and years in E2. For irregular cash flows, use `=XIRR(values, dates)`.

**Is rate of return the same as yield?** No. Yield usually refers to the
income an investment pays (such as dividends or interest) as a percentage
of its price. Rate of return includes both income and the change in value.

**Can a rate of return be negative?** Yes. If the ending value plus income
is less than what you invested, the return is negative.
