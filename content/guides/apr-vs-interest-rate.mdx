Every loan advertisement shows an interest rate, and most show an APR too —
and they're almost never the same number. Knowing what separates them is the
difference between comparing loans accurately and being misled by whichever
number happens to look better.

## What the Interest Rate Tells You

The interest rate is what your monthly payment is based on. It's the rate
applied to your outstanding balance to calculate the interest portion of each
payment. If two loans have the same amount, term, and interest rate, they'll
have the same monthly payment — regardless of any fees attached to either
one.

## What APR Adds

Annual Percentage Rate folds in certain upfront fees — commonly an
origination fee — and expresses the total cost of borrowing as an equivalent
annual rate. Because you pay interest on the full loan amount but don't
receive the full amount in cash when a fee is deducted upfront, the true cost
of borrowing is higher than the interest rate alone suggests. APR is the
number designed to reflect that.

## A Concrete Example

Consider a $10,000 loan at 8% interest over 3 years with no fee: the interest
rate and the APR are effectively the same. Now add a $500 origination fee
deducted from the loan proceeds. You still owe $10,000 and still make the same
payment calculated on $10,000 at 8% — but you only received $9,500. Measured
against what you actually got, the true annual cost is higher than 8%, and
that higher figure is the APR.

## Why This Matters When Comparing Loans

A loan with a lower interest rate but a large origination fee can end up more
expensive than a loan with a slightly higher rate and no fee — especially
over a shorter term, where the fee makes up a bigger share of the total cost.
Comparing by interest rate alone can lead to the wrong choice; comparing by
APR accounts for the fee automatically.

## Where This Doesn't Apply the Same Way

Credit cards quote APR too, but typically without an origination fee baked
in, so the "interest rate vs. APR" gap described here is mainly relevant to
instalment loans like mortgages, auto loans, and personal loans.
