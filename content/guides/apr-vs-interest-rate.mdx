Every loan advertisement shows an interest rate, and most show an APR too —
and they're almost never the same number. Knowing what separates them is the
difference between comparing loans accurately and being misled by whichever
number happens to look better. This guide works through the math that turns
fees into an APR, what the law counts as a finance charge, and where APR can
point you the wrong way.

## What the Interest Rate Tells You

The interest rate is what your monthly payment is based on. It's the rate
applied to your outstanding balance to calculate the interest portion of each
payment. If two loans have the same amount, term, and interest rate, they'll
have the same monthly payment — regardless of any fees attached to either
one.

## What APR Adds

Annual Percentage Rate folds in certain upfront fees — commonly an
origination fee — and expresses the total cost of borrowing as an equivalent
annual rate. Because you pay interest on the full loan amount but don't
receive the full amount in cash when a fee is deducted upfront, the true cost
of borrowing is higher than the interest rate alone suggests. APR is the
number designed to reflect that.

In the U.S., APR is defined by the Truth in Lending Act and its implementing
rule, **Regulation Z**, which requires lenders to disclose it on consumer
loans.

## A Concrete Example

Consider a $10,000 loan at 8% interest over 3 years with no fee: the interest
rate and the APR are effectively the same. Now add a $500 origination fee
deducted from the loan proceeds. You still owe $10,000 and still make the same
payment calculated on $10,000 at 8% — but you only received $9,500. Measured
against what you actually got, the true annual cost is higher than 8%, and
that higher figure is the APR.

## How APR Is Actually Calculated

Regulation Z defines APR as the rate that makes the present value of your
payment schedule equal to the **amount financed** — roughly, the loan amount
minus prepaid finance charges such as a fee deducted at closing. There's no
closed-form formula; it's solved by trial and error or with a financial
calculator.

### Step 1: Find the Payment and the Amount Financed

For the $10,000, 8%, 36-month loan above, the standard amortization formula
gives:

```
Payment = $10,000 × (0.08 ÷ 12) ÷ (1 − (1 + 0.08 ÷ 12)^−36) = $313.36
```

Over 36 months you pay $313.36 × 36 = **$11,281.09**, of which **$1,281.09**
is interest. With the $500 fee deducted, the amount financed is **$9,500**.

### Step 2: Solve for the Rate

Find the annual rate at which 36 monthly payments of $313.36 have a present
value of exactly $9,500:

| Trial annual rate | Present value of 36 payments |
| --- | --- |
| 8.00% | $10,000 |
| 11.00% | about $9,572 |
| 11.52% | about $9,500 |
| 12.00% | about $9,435 |

The rate that balances the equation is about **11.52%** — the APR. The fee
costs you the equivalent of more than three and a half percentage points a
year, even though the contract rate is 8%. The interest plus the fee,
$1,781.09, is what Regulation Z calls the finance charge.

### The Term Changes the Answer

The same $500 fee looks very different over different terms:

| Term | Monthly payment | APR with $500 fee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 year | $869.88 | 17.76% |
| 3 years | $313.36 | 11.52% |
| 5 years | $202.76 | 10.20% |

A fixed-dollar fee spread across fewer payments pushes the APR up much more.

## What Counts as a Finance Charge

APR can only be as complete as the costs fed into it. Regulation Z defines
the **finance charge** as charges you pay as a condition of getting credit.
At a high level, it includes:

- **Interest**
- **Points, loan fees, assumption fees, and finder's fees**
- **Premiums for insurance that protects the lender** against your default,
  such as mortgage insurance

For loans secured by real estate, several common closing costs are
**excluded**: title examination and title insurance, document preparation
and notary fees, appraisal and credit report fees, and amounts paid into
escrow for taxes and insurance. Application fees charged to every applicant
and points paid by the seller are excluded too.

So a mortgage APR captures the lender's charges but not all the cash you'll
need at closing. Compare the closing cost section of each Loan Estimate
alongside the APR.

## A Mortgage Example: Points vs. No Points

Suppose you're borrowing **$300,000 on a 30-year fixed loan** with two
offers. The rates are examples chosen for the arithmetic, not market quotes:

| | Loan A | Loan B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interest rate | 6.25% | 6.50% |
| Discount points | 2 points ($6,000) | None |
| Origination fee | $1,500 | $1,500 |
| Monthly principal and interest | $1,847.15 | $1,896.20 |
| APR | **6.49%** | **6.55%** |

Loan A has the lower APR, and held for the full 30 years it's cheaper. But
APR assumes you keep the loan to maturity, and many people sell or refinance
sooner. Here's the total of interest plus upfront charges if each loan is
paid off early:

| Paid off after | Loan A cost | Loan B cost | Cheaper option |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 years | $98,341 | $96,105 | B, by $2,236 |
| 10 years | $181,871 | $183,373 | A, by $1,502 |
| 30 years | $372,475 | $384,133 | A, by $11,659 |

The break-even in this example is **8 years** (96 months). If you expect to
move or refinance sooner, the upfront fees deserve more weight than the APR
gives them. The [mortgage calculator](/calculators/mortgage-calculator)
shows the payment on each offer, and the [refinance
calculator](/calculators/refinance-calculator) runs the same break-even math
when you're replacing an existing loan.

## APR vs. APY

On the savings side, banks quote **annual percentage yield (APY)**, defined
under the Truth in Savings Act. APR is a simple annual rate — 6% APR charged
monthly is 0.5% a month — while APY includes the effect of interest earning
interest within the year:

```
APY = (1 + APR ÷ 12)^12 − 1
```

| APR | APY, monthly compounding | APY, daily compounding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6% | 6.17% | 6.18% |
| 24% | 26.82% | 27.11% |

Because APR leaves out compounding, it understates the true annual cost of
a balance that compounds. When comparing a loan to a savings account, or a
credit card to an investment return, convert both to the same basis first.

## Why This Matters When Comparing Loans

A loan with a lower interest rate but a large origination fee can end up more
expensive than a loan with a slightly higher rate and no fee — especially
over a shorter term, where the fee makes up a bigger share of the total cost.
Comparing by interest rate alone can lead to the wrong choice; comparing by
APR accounts for the fee automatically.

APR comparisons work best between loans of the same amount and term. On an
adjustable-rate loan, the APR rests on assumptions about future rates and
can't predict what you'll actually pay.

## Where This Doesn't Apply the Same Way

Credit cards quote APR too, but typically without an origination fee baked
in, so the "interest rate vs. APR" gap described here is mainly relevant to
installment loans like mortgages, auto loans, and personal loans. The
[credit card payoff calculator](/calculators/credit-card-payoff-calculator)
shows what a card's APR costs over time.

## Putting It Together

The interest rate sets your payment; the APR folds in the lender's fees and
restates the cost as the rate at which your payments equal the money you
actually received. It's the best single number for comparing loans of the
same size and term, as long as you remember it assumes you'll keep the loan
to the end and leaves out some closing costs.

Use the [loan calculator](/calculators/loan-calculator) to see the exact gap

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is APR or interest rate more important?** They answer different questions.
The interest rate sets your monthly payment, while APR adds certain lender
fees to show the overall cost of borrowing. APR is the better number for
comparing loans of the same amount and term, as long as you expect to keep
the loan to the end.

**Why is my APR higher than my interest rate?** Because APR includes finance
charges paid to get the loan, such as origination fees, points, and mortgage
insurance. In the example above, a $500 fee on a $10,000, 3-year loan at 8%
raises the APR to about 11.52%. The shorter the term, the more a fee pushes
the APR up.

**Does APR include closing costs?** Only some of them. On a mortgage, APR
includes lender charges like points and loan fees, but excludes costs such as
title insurance, appraisal and credit report fees, document preparation and
notary fees, and escrow deposits. Compare the closing cost section of each
Loan Estimate alongside the APR.

**Is a credit card's APR the same as its interest rate?** For most purchases,
yes. Cards usually don't charge an origination fee, so the APR is the rate
used to calculate interest. Because interest compounds, the true annual cost
is higher: a 24% APR works out to about 26.82% with monthly compounding. The
[credit card payoff calculator](/calculators/credit-card-payoff-calculator)
shows what that rate costs over time.
