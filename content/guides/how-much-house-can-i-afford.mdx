Lenders and financial planners don't answer "how much house can I afford"
with a single number — they use ratios that connect your income, your
existing debt, and your down payment. Understanding those ratios lets you
answer the question yourself, before a lender does it for you. This guide
applies the 28/36 rule at several incomes, shows what the down payment and
PMI change, adds up the full cost of owning, and stress-tests the result.

## The Front-End Ratio: Housing Cost vs. Income

The front-end ratio compares your total monthly housing cost — principal,
interest, property tax, insurance, and any HOA fee — to your gross monthly
income. A commonly cited guideline caps this around 28%, though conventional
loans can sometimes go higher depending on the lender and your overall
financial profile. On a $7,000 monthly gross income, a 28% front-end ratio
points to roughly $1,960 a month available for housing costs combined.

## The Back-End Ratio: Total Debt vs. Income

The back-end ratio, also called debt-to-income (DTI), adds your other debt
payments — car loans, student loans, credit card minimums — on top of
housing costs, then compares the total to gross income. The classic
guideline is 36%, and many loan programs allow 43% or more depending on the
loan type and your credit profile. This ratio is often the binding
constraint: a low front-end ratio doesn't help if other debt pushes your
back-end ratio too high.

For what counts as debt, see [how to calculate your debt-to-income
ratio](/guides/how-to-calculate-debt-to-income-ratio) or the
[debt-to-income calculator](/calculators/debt-to-income-calculator).

## The 28/36 Rule at Different Incomes

Your housing budget is the **lower** of two limits:

```
Housing budget = lesser of:
  28% × gross monthly income
  36% × gross monthly income − other monthly debt payments
```

Here's the same **$600 a month** of other debt — say, a car payment and a
student loan — at four household incomes:

| Annual income | 28% limit | 36% limit minus $600 | Housing budget |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $50,000 | $1,167 | $900 | **$900** |
| $80,000 | $1,867 | $1,800 | **$1,800** |
| $120,000 | $2,800 | $3,000 | **$2,800** |
| $160,000 | $3,733 | $4,200 | **$3,733** |

At $50,000 and $80,000, the back-end limit binds: the $600 in other debt eats
into the housing budget dollar for dollar. At $120,000 and above, the 28% cap
takes over. The switch happens where other debt equals 8% of gross income, the
gap between 36% and 28%. Above that line, paying off debt raises your housing
budget; at $80,000, eliminating just $67 of monthly debt would lift it from
$1,800 to about $1,867. The [auto loan
calculator](/calculators/auto-loan-calculator) and [credit card payoff
calculator](/calculators/credit-card-payoff-calculator) help you see how fast
those payments can go away.

The $60,000 case, including how rates and property taxes shift the answer,
is worked through in [how much house can I afford on
$60K](/guides/how-much-house-can-i-afford-on-60k). If you've saved $60,000
rather than earn it, see [whether $60,000 is a good down
payment](/guides/is-60000-a-good-down-payment).

### From Budget to Home Price

Converting a budget into a price takes assumptions. These are **examples**
— yours will differ:

- 30-year fixed mortgage at **7.00%** (an example rate, not a quote)
- Property taxes of **1.0%** of the home's value per year
- Homeowners insurance of **0.5%** of the home's value per year
- PMI of **0.5%** of the loan amount per year when less than 20% is down
- No HOA dues

| Housing budget | Price with 10% down | Price with 20% down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $900 | about $118,000 | about $137,000 |
| $1,800 | about $236,000 | about $274,000 |
| $2,800 | about $368,000 | about $426,000 |
| $3,733 | about $490,000 | about $568,000 |

## The Down Payment's Double Effect

A larger down payment does two things simultaneously: it reduces the loan
amount (and therefore the monthly principal-and-interest payment), and — if
it reaches 20% of the home price — it typically eliminates private mortgage
insurance (PMI), an added monthly cost. Both effects push more home price
into reach for the same monthly budget.

### A $400,000 Home at Three Down Payments

Using the same example assumptions:

| | 5% down | 10% down | 20% down |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Down payment | $20,000 | $40,000 | $80,000 |
| Loan amount | $380,000 | $360,000 | $320,000 |
| Principal and interest | $2,528.15 | $2,395.09 | $2,128.97 |
| Property taxes | $333.33 | $333.33 | $333.33 |
| Homeowners insurance | $166.67 | $166.67 | $166.67 |
| PMI | $158.33 | $150.00 | $0 |
| **Monthly total** | **$3,186.48** | **$3,045.09** | **$2,628.97** |

Going from 10% to 20% down costs $40,000 more in cash and lowers the
monthly payment by **$416.12** — about $266 of that from the smaller loan
and $150 from dropping PMI.

### PMI Doesn't Last Forever

On conventional loans, the Homeowners Protection Act lets you ask the lender
to cancel PMI once your balance is scheduled to reach **80%** of the home's
original value, and requires it to end automatically at **78%**, provided
you're current on payments. Following the original amortization schedule in
this example:

| Down payment | Reaches 80% of original value | Automatic cancellation at 78% |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5% | Month 130 (about 10.8 years) | Month 142 (about 11.8 years) |
| 10% | Month 101 (about 8.4 years) | Month 115 (about 9.6 years) |

With 10% down, PMI at $150 a month until automatic cancellation adds up to
about **$17,250**; with 5% down, about **$22,483**. Extra principal payments
reach those thresholds sooner. FHA loans follow different mortgage insurance
rules.

## The Full Cost of Ownership

Property tax, home insurance, HOA fees, and maintenance are easy to
underestimate when focusing on the loan itself, but they're a real and
ongoing part of the cost of owning. A home that looks affordable based on
principal and interest alone can strain a budget once these are added in.

Taking the $400,000 home with 10% down from above, here's how the monthly
cost builds up:

| Layer | Monthly | Running total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Principal and interest | $2,395.09 | $2,395.09 |
| Property taxes | $333.33 | $2,728.42 |
| Homeowners insurance | $166.67 | $2,895.09 |
| PMI | $150.00 | $3,045.09 |
| Maintenance reserve (1% of value a year) | $333.33 | $3,378.42 |

The loan payment alone is about 71% of the true monthly cost. The 1%
maintenance figure is a planning assumption, not a measured average — older
homes and harsher climates can need more, and repairs arrive in lumps.

Lenders count the first four lines in your ratios; keeping that $3,045.09 at
28% of gross income takes about **$130,500** a year. Maintenance appears in
no lender ratio, which is why it's easy to forget. Also budget for HOA dues
if any, higher utilities, closing costs, and escrow payments that can rise
every year even on a fixed-rate loan.

## Stress-Testing Your Budget

Take the $120,000 household from above, buying a home at about **$426,000**
with 20% down — a monthly housing payment of about **$2,800**, exactly 28% of
gross income, plus $600 in other debt.

| Scenario | Monthly housing | Housing ratio | Total ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base case | $2,799.85 | 28.0% | 34.0% |
| Rate rises from 7% to 8% before you lock | $3,033.17 | 30.3% | 36.3% |
| Taxes and insurance rise 25% | $2,932.98 | 29.3% | 35.3% |
| Household income falls 20% to $96,000 | $2,799.85 | 35.0% | 42.5% |

None of these scenarios is extreme. Each pushes housing past 28%, and two
push total debt past 36%. A few ways to build in margin:

- **Target less than 28%.** Budgeting 25% of gross income — $2,500 a month
  here — supports a home of about $380,000 under the same assumptions.
- **Rerun the payment at a higher rate** in the [mortgage
  calculator](/calculators/mortgage-calculator). If one point breaks the
  budget, you're shopping at the edge.
- **Keep reserves after closing.** Three months of this household's housing
  and debt payments is about $10,200; six months is about $20,400.
- **Test on take-home pay.** Bills are paid from net pay, alongside savings,
  child care, and everything else the ratios ignore.

Fannie Mae's automated underwriting allows total DTI up to 50% on
conventional loans, but an approval shows what a lender allows, not what
your budget can comfortably carry.

## Putting It Together

Start with your gross monthly income, apply a front-end ratio to estimate a
housing budget, check that budget against your back-end ratio with existing
debt included, and then work backward to a home price using your planned down
payment and current mortgage rates. Then add the costs lenders don't count
and test whether the payment survives higher rates, taxes, or lower income.

The [mortgage calculator](/calculators/mortgage-calculator) will show you the
exact monthly payment — including tax, insurance, and PMI — for any home
price and down payment combination, so you can test different scenarios
against your own budget directly.
