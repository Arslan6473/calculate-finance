Paying a little extra on your mortgage is one of the simplest ways to save
money on a home, and the savings are often much larger than people expect.
An extra $100 or $200 a month can cut years off a 30-year loan and save tens
of thousands of dollars in interest. This guide explains why extra payments
work so well, shows exactly how much different strategies save on the same
loan, and covers the practical details — and tradeoffs — to consider before
you start.

## Why Extra Payments Work

Each month, your lender charges interest on your **current balance**. Your
regular payment covers that interest first, and whatever is left reduces the
principal. Early in a 30-year loan, interest takes most of the payment.

Take a **$300,000** loan at **7.00%** for 30 years. The monthly principal and
interest payment is **$1,995.91**. In the first month:

```
Interest = $300,000 × 7.00% ÷ 12 = $1,750.00
Principal = $1,995.91 − $1,750.00 = $245.91
```

Nearly 88% of that first payment is interest. Only about $246 reduces what
you owe.

Now suppose you add $200 to that payment. The entire extra $200 goes to
principal, because the interest for the month has already been covered. That
lowers the balance the next month's interest is calculated on — and the
month after that, and every month for the rest of the loan. Each extra
dollar of principal you pay today stops generating interest for as long as
the loan would otherwise have lasted.

Because the required payment on a fixed-rate loan doesn't change, the
interest you avoid shows up as additional principal in every future payment.
The loan pays itself down faster and faster, and it ends early.

## What Different Strategies Save

All of the examples below use the same $300,000, 30-year loan at 7.00%. With
no extra payments, you'd make 360 payments and pay **$418,527** in total
interest. These figures come from a month-by-month amortization of the loan;
the 7.00% rate is an example, and your own savings depend on your balance,
rate, and remaining term.

### Extra Every Month

| Extra per month | Payoff time | Time saved | Interest saved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $100 | 25 years, 10 months | 4 years, 2 months | $69,338 |
| $200 | 22 years, 11 months | 7 years, 1 month | $116,640 |
| $500 | 17 years, 4 months | 12 years, 8 months | $200,235 |

An extra $100 a month adds up to roughly $31,000 of additional principal over
the shortened life of the loan, and it saves more than twice that in
interest. At $500 a month, you save about $200,000 and own the home outright
more than a decade early.

### One Extra Payment a Year

Making the equivalent of **13 payments a year** instead of 12 is a popular
approach. You can do it by adding one-twelfth of your payment — here,
**$166.33** — to every monthly payment:

| Strategy | Payoff time | Time saved | Interest saved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One extra payment a year | 23 years, 9 months | 6 years, 3 months | $102,424 |

This is the idea behind **biweekly payment plans**: paying half the monthly
amount every two weeks results in 26 half-payments, or 13 full payments, a
year. You can get the same effect on your own by adding one-twelfth of a
payment each month. Before signing up for a biweekly program, check whether
it charges fees and whether the money is actually applied to your loan every
two weeks or held until the full payment is due.

### A One-Time Lump Sum

Windfalls such as a bonus, tax refund, or inheritance can go straight to
principal. Here's what a single **$10,000** prepayment does, depending on
when you make it:

| When you pay $10,000 | Time saved | Interest saved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Month 1 | 3 years | $62,662 |
| Year 5 (month 60) | 2 years, 2 months | $43,092 |
| Year 10 (month 120) | 1 year, 7 months | $28,287 |

## Earlier Is Better

The lump-sum table shows the most important rule of prepaying: **timing
matters**. The same $10,000 saves more than twice as much interest in month 1
as in year 10, because it has more years in which to avoid interest.

The same is true of monthly extras. An extra $200 a month from the start
saves $116,640. If you wait until year 11 to begin paying that same $200 a
month, you save **$44,243** and finish about 3½ years early — still worth
doing, but a much smaller payoff.

That doesn't mean it's ever too late. Any extra principal saves the interest
it would have accrued for the rest of the loan. It just means a given dollar
goes furthest in the early years.

## Your Guaranteed Return

Paying down a 7.00% mortgage is equivalent to earning a **guaranteed 7.00%**
on that money, because every dollar of principal you eliminate stops costing
you 7.00% a year. There's no market risk and no tax on the "return."

That's a useful way to compare prepaying against other uses of the money:

- **Pay off higher-rate debt first.** A credit card at 20% or more is a far
  better target than a mortgage at 7%.
- **Capture any employer retirement match** before prepaying. A match is an
  immediate return that's hard to beat.
- **Keep an emergency fund.** Money you send to your mortgage is hard to get
  back out quickly. You can't withdraw extra principal if you lose your job;
  you'd need to sell, refinance, or borrow against the home.
- **Compare with investing.** Long-term stock market returns may be higher
  than your mortgage rate, but they're not guaranteed and they fluctuate. The
  lower your mortgage rate, the more the comparison favors investing; the
  higher the rate, the more prepaying looks attractive.

If you itemize deductions on your federal return, the mortgage interest
deduction lowers your effective rate somewhat. Many households take the
standard deduction, though, in which case the deduction doesn't change the
math at all.

## Practical Details

### Make Sure It Goes to Principal

When you send extra money, tell your servicer to apply it to **principal**.
Otherwise, some servicers may treat it as an early payment of next month's
bill instead of a reduction in your balance. Check your next statement to
confirm the balance dropped by the full extra amount.

### Your Payment Won't Go Down

On a standard fixed-rate mortgage, extra payments shorten the loan; they
don't lower the required monthly payment. If you'd rather have a lower
payment after a large prepayment, ask your lender whether it offers a
**recast**, which recalculates the payment on the new balance over the
remaining term, usually for a fee.

### Check for a Prepayment Penalty

Many mortgages have no penalty for paying early, but some do. Your loan
documents will say whether a penalty applies and under what conditions.

### Prepaying Can End PMI Sooner

If you put down less than 20% on a conventional loan, you're likely paying
private mortgage insurance until you build up enough equity. Extra principal
payments build equity faster, which can shorten the time until PMI can be
removed — an additional saving on top of the interest.

## When Extra Payments Make the Most Sense

- You have a stable emergency fund and no high-interest debt.
- You're already saving enough for retirement.
- Your mortgage rate is relatively high.
- You plan to stay in the home for many years.
- You value being debt-free, especially as retirement approaches.

If you might sell within a few years, prepaying still saves interest and
leaves you with more equity at sale, but the savings are smaller because the
loan won't run long enough for the full effect.

## Putting It Together

Extra mortgage payments save interest because every dollar goes straight to
principal and stops accruing interest for the rest of the loan. On a
$300,000 loan at 7.00%, $200 extra a month saves about $117,000 and seven
years, and one extra payment a year saves about $102,000. Start as early as
you can, make sure the money is applied to principal, and weigh prepaying
against higher-rate debt, retirement savings, and your emergency fund.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does an extra $100 a month save on a mortgage?** On a $300,000,
30-year loan at 7.00%, an extra $100 a month saves $69,338 in interest and
pays the loan off 4 years and 2 months early. Your savings depend on your
balance, rate, and remaining term; the [mortgage
calculator](/calculators/mortgage-calculator) shows your own numbers.

**Do biweekly mortgage payments save money?** Yes, because 26 half-payments a
year add up to 13 full payments instead of 12. On the same $300,000 loan,
that saves $102,424 and 6 years and 3 months. You can get the same effect by
adding one-twelfth of your payment each month, and it's worth checking any
biweekly program for fees.

**Is it better to pay off your mortgage early or invest?** It depends on your
rate and priorities. Prepaying earns a guaranteed return equal to your
mortgage rate, while investing may earn more but isn't guaranteed. Paying off
higher-rate debt, capturing an employer match, and keeping an emergency fund
usually come first.

**Is it too late to make extra mortgage payments?** No. Any extra principal
saves the interest it would have accrued for the rest of the loan, though a
given dollar goes furthest early on. On the example loan, starting $200 a
month in year 11 still saves $44,243 and ends the loan about 3½ years early.
