Sixty thousand dollars is a lot of money to save, and it's a strong start
on a home purchase. Whether it's a *good* down payment depends almost
entirely on the price of the home you're buying. On a $300,000 house it's a
full 20%; on a $750,000 house it's 8%. This guide shows what $60,000 covers
at different prices, what it means for your monthly payment and mortgage
insurance, and how to decide whether to put all of it down.

## The Short Answer

**$60,000 is a good down payment for homes priced up to about $300,000,
where it reaches the 20% mark that avoids private mortgage insurance on a
conventional loan.** Above that, it's still a solid down payment — well
above the 3% to 3.5% minimums on many loan programs — but you'll likely pay
mortgage insurance, and the monthly payment rises quickly with the price.

You also need money beyond the down payment for closing costs, moving, and
an emergency fund. If $60,000 is everything you have, the right target price
is lower than the math alone suggests.

## What Percentage Is $60,000?

```
Down payment percentage = Down payment ÷ Home price × 100
```

| Home price | $60,000 as a down payment | Loan amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $200,000 | 30.0% | $140,000 |
| $300,000 | 20.0% | $240,000 |
| $400,000 | 15.0% | $340,000 |
| $500,000 | 12.0% | $440,000 |
| $600,000 | 10.0% | $540,000 |
| $750,000 | 8.0% | $690,000 |

The highest price at which $60,000 is a 20% down payment is **$300,000**.

## Why 20% Matters

On a conventional mortgage, putting down less than 20% usually means paying
**private mortgage insurance (PMI)**. PMI protects the lender, not you, and
it's added to your monthly payment until you build 20% equity.

The good news is that PMI isn't permanent. Under federal rules, you can ask
your servicer to cancel it once your balance is scheduled to reach 80% of the
home's original value, and the servicer generally must end it automatically
at 78%, as long as you're current on payments. Extra payments toward
principal get you there sooner.

FHA loans work differently: they charge their own mortgage insurance
premiums, which on many loans last for the life of the loan regardless of
how much you put down.

## What $60,000 Down Means for Your Monthly Payment

Here's the estimated monthly housing payment at three prices, using example
assumptions: a 30-year fixed rate of **6.5%**, property taxes of 1% of the
price a year, $150 a month for homeowners insurance, and PMI of 0.5% of the
loan a year when the down payment is under 20%. Your actual rate, taxes,
insurance, and PMI will differ.

| | $300,000 home | $400,000 home | $500,000 home |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Down payment | 20% | 15% | 12% |
| Loan amount | $240,000 | $340,000 | $440,000 |
| Principal and interest | $1,517 | $2,149 | $2,781 |
| PMI | $0 | $142 | $183 |
| Property taxes | $250 | $333 | $417 |
| Insurance | $150 | $150 | $150 |
| **Total monthly payment** | **$1,917** | **$2,774** | **$3,531** |
| Income for a 28% housing ratio | ≈ $82,000 | ≈ $119,000 | ≈ $151,000 |

The last row uses the common guideline of keeping the housing payment at or
below 28% of gross income. With $60,000 down, a household earning around
$82,000 a year fits a $300,000 home under that guideline; a $500,000 home
calls for about $151,000.

## Should You Put All $60,000 Down?

Not necessarily. A bigger down payment lowers your payment, your interest
costs, and your PMI — but it also ties up cash you might need.

Compare putting $60,000 versus $30,000 down on a $300,000 home with the same
assumptions:

| | $60,000 down (20%) | $30,000 down (10%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loan amount | $240,000 | $270,000 |
| Monthly payment (incl. taxes, insurance, PMI) | $1,917 | $2,219 |
| PMI | None | $113 a month until about 20% equity |
| Interest over 30 years | ≈ $306,000 | ≈ $344,000 |
| Cash kept in reserve | $0 | $30,000 |

Putting all $60,000 down saves about $300 a month and roughly $38,000 of
interest over the full term. Keeping $30,000 in the bank gives you a cushion
for repairs, job loss, or other emergencies — and with regular payments alone,
the 10%-down loan reaches 80% of the original value in about eight years,
when PMI can be dropped.

### Keep Enough for Everything Else

Before deciding how much to put down, set aside money for:

- **Closing costs.** Lender fees, title insurance, appraisal, prepaid taxes
  and insurance. These commonly run a few percent of the loan amount.
- **Moving and immediate repairs.** New homes almost always need something.
- **An emergency fund.** Three to six months of essential expenses —
  including the new mortgage payment. See [how much to keep in an emergency
  fund](/guides/how-much-emergency-fund).
- **Lender reserves.** Some loans require you to show a few months of
  payments in savings after closing.

If those costs come out of the same $60,000, your actual down payment may be
closer to $40,000 or $45,000.

## When $60,000 Down Makes Sense

- **You're buying in a moderately priced market,** where $60,000 reaches or
  comes close to 20%.
- **You already have a separate emergency fund** and money for closing
  costs.
- **You want the lowest possible payment** and the least interest.

## When to Put Down Less

- **The $60,000 is all your savings.** Keeping a cushion protects you from
  having to borrow on credit cards after a surprise repair.
- **You have high-interest debt.** Paying off a credit card at 24% usually
  beats reducing a mortgage at 6.5%.
- **You qualify for a low-down-payment program** with favorable terms, such
  as a VA loan or a first-time buyer program, and would rather keep cash.

## Putting It Together

$60,000 is a 20% down payment on a $300,000 home, enough to avoid PMI on a
conventional loan. On pricier homes it's still a substantial down payment,
but you'll likely pay mortgage insurance, and the monthly payment grows
quickly. Make sure the down payment leaves enough for closing costs and an
emergency fund, and judge the purchase by the monthly payment you can carry
comfortably, not just the amount you can put down.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much house can I buy with $60,000 down?** It depends on your income,
debts, and interest rate more than the down payment. With 20% down and no
PMI, $60,000 fits a $300,000 home. Lenders will approve higher prices if your
income supports the payment.

**Is it better to put 20% down or keep cash?** Twenty percent down avoids PMI
and lowers your payment, but it isn't worth emptying your savings. Many
buyers are better off putting down less and keeping a solid emergency fund.

**What's the minimum down payment on a house?** Some conventional loans allow
3% down, FHA loans allow 3.5% with qualifying credit, and VA and USDA loans
can require no down payment for eligible borrowers.

**Does a bigger down payment get a lower interest rate?** Often, yes. Lenders
price loans partly on the loan-to-value ratio, so a larger down payment can
qualify for a better rate or lower mortgage insurance.
