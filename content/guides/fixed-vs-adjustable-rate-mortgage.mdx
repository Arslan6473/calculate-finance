One of the first choices you make when you finance a home is whether the
interest rate should be locked for the life of the loan or allowed to change
over time. A **fixed-rate mortgage** keeps the same rate and the same
principal-and-interest payment until the loan is paid off. An
**adjustable-rate mortgage** (ARM) starts with a rate that's fixed for a few
years — usually lower than a comparable fixed rate — and then resets
periodically based on market rates. This guide explains how ARMs are built,
what the numbers in a name like "5/6" mean, how rate caps limit the damage,
and how to decide which type fits your situation.

## Fixed vs. Adjustable Rate at a Glance

| | Fixed-rate mortgage | Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interest rate | Same for the life of the loan | Fixed for an initial period, then resets |
| Starting rate | Usually higher | Usually lower |
| Monthly payment | Principal and interest never change | Can rise or fall after the fixed period |
| Risk of rising rates | Lender carries it | You carry it, within the caps |
| Best fit | Staying long-term, tight budget, wants certainty | Moving or refinancing before the reset, room in the budget |

## How a Fixed-Rate Mortgage Works

A fixed-rate mortgage is the simple case. The interest rate set at closing
never changes, so the monthly principal and interest stay the same for the
entire term — typically 30 or 15 years. Your total housing payment can still
rise if property taxes or insurance premiums go up, but the loan part won't.

The tradeoff is price. Lenders take on the risk that rates will rise while
your loan sits at its original rate, and they usually charge for that with a
somewhat higher starting rate than an ARM's introductory rate.

## How an Adjustable-Rate Mortgage Works

An ARM has two phases:

1. **The initial fixed period.** The rate is locked for a set number of
   years, commonly 3, 5, 7, or 10.
2. **The adjustment period.** After that, the rate resets at regular
   intervals for the rest of the loan.

### Reading the Name

ARM names tell you both phases. The first number is the length of the
initial fixed period in years. The second number describes how often the
rate adjusts afterward:

- A **5/1 ARM** is fixed for five years, then adjusts once a year.
- A **5/6 ARM** is fixed for five years, then adjusts every six months.
- A **7/6** or **10/6 ARM** follows the same pattern with a seven- or
  ten-year fixed period.

Many newer conventional ARMs use the six-month adjustment schedule.

### Index Plus Margin

When an ARM adjusts, the new rate isn't set at the lender's discretion. It's
calculated from a formula written into your loan documents:

```
New rate = Index + Margin
```

The **index** is a published benchmark rate that moves with the market. For
ARMs that Fannie Mae buys, the index is the 30-day average of the Secured
Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR), published by the Federal Reserve Bank of New
York. SOFR reflects the cost of borrowing cash overnight using Treasury
securities as collateral.

The **margin** is a fixed number of percentage points added to the index.
It's set when you take out the loan and doesn't change. Fannie Mae, for
example, caps the margin on the loans it buys at 3 percentage points. The
sum of the index and margin — the **fully indexed rate** — is usually rounded
to the nearest one-eighth of a percent.

### Rate Caps

Caps limit how far the rate can move. They're usually written as three
numbers, such as **2/1/5**:

- **Initial adjustment cap (2):** the most the rate can change at the first
  reset.
- **Periodic cap (1):** the most it can change at each later reset.
- **Lifetime cap (5):** the most it can ever rise above the starting rate.

Caps are the single most important feature to understand before choosing an
ARM, because they define your worst case. Under Fannie Mae's rules, lifetime
caps limit increases, while per-adjustment caps apply to both increases and
decreases — so a cap can also slow how fast your rate falls.

## A Worked Example

Suppose you're borrowing **$350,000** for 30 years and you're offered two
loans. These rates are examples for illustration; actual rates change
constantly, and Freddie Mac publishes weekly national averages for fixed-rate
mortgages if you want a current benchmark.

- A **30-year fixed** at **7.00%**
- A **5/6 ARM** at **6.25%** for the first five years, with **2/1/5** caps

### The First Five Years

| | 30-year fixed | 5/6 ARM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly principal and interest | $2,328.56 | $2,155.01 |
| Interest paid in years 1–5 | $119,174 | $105,981 |
| Balance after 5 years | $329,461 | $326,680 |

The ARM saves **$173.55 a month**, or about **$10,413** over the five-year
fixed period. Because more of each payment goes to principal at the lower
rate, the ARM's balance is also about $2,780 lower at the end of year five.

### What Happens at the First Reset

At month 61, the ARM's rate becomes the index plus the margin, limited by the
caps. Assume a margin of 2.75%. The new payment is calculated on the
remaining $326,680 balance over the remaining 25 years:

| Scenario at reset | New rate | New payment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Index falls to 2.75% | 5.50% | $2,006 |
| Index is 3.50% | 6.25% | $2,155 |
| Index rises to 4.75% | 7.50% | $2,414 |
| Index jumps; first cap applies | 8.25% | $2,576 |

If rates fall, the ARM's payment drops and the gap with the fixed loan
widens. If the index rises to 4.75%, the payment climbs about $259 a month
and ends up around $85 above the fixed loan's payment.

### The Worst Case

The caps tell you exactly how bad it can get. With 2/1/5 caps starting at
6.25%, the rate can rise to 8.25% at the first reset, then by up to one point
every six months, and never above 11.25%:

| Months | Maximum rate | Maximum payment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–60 | 6.25% | $2,155 |
| 61–66 | 8.25% | $2,576 |
| 67–72 | 9.25% | $2,795 |
| 73–78 | 10.25% | $3,019 |
| 79 onward | 11.25% | $3,246 |

In this worst case, the payment rises by about **$1,091 a month** — roughly
50% — within two years of the first reset. That's the risk you're accepting
in exchange for the lower starting payment. It's not the expected outcome,
but you should be able to survive it, either by affording the payment or by
having a realistic plan to sell or refinance.

## When an ARM Can Make Sense

An ARM tends to fit when one or more of these is true:

- **You're confident you'll sell or move before the fixed period ends.** If
  you sell in year four, you collect the lower rate and never see a reset.
- **You expect your income to rise substantially**, so a higher future
  payment would still be comfortable.
- **The gap between ARM and fixed rates is wide**, making the early savings
  meaningful.
- **You have a cushion** of savings that could absorb a few years of higher
  payments.

## When a Fixed Rate Is the Better Choice

A fixed rate usually wins when:

- **You plan to stay in the home for many years**, well past the initial
  fixed period.
- **Your budget is already tight**, and a payment increase of a few hundred
  dollars would cause real hardship.
- **The rate difference is small**, so you're taking on reset risk for
  little savings.
- **You value predictability.** A payment that never changes makes long-term
  budgeting easier.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"I'll just refinance before it resets."** That may work, but it depends
  on rates, home values, your credit, and your income at that future date.
  Refinancing also has closing costs. Treat it as an option, not a
  guarantee.
- **"The cap protects me."** Caps limit how fast and how far the rate rises,
  but a 5-point lifetime cap still allows a very large payment increase.
- **"Lenders qualify me for the worst case."** Not necessarily. For ARMs
  with initial fixed periods of five years or less, Fannie Mae requires
  lenders to qualify borrowers using a higher qualifying rate to limit
  payment shock, but that's still not the full lifetime maximum.
- **"Fixed is always more expensive."** If rates fall, a fixed-rate borrower
  can refinance; if they rise, the fixed borrower is protected. That
  flexibility has real value.

## Questions to Ask a Lender

- What is the index, and what is the margin?
- What are the initial, periodic, and lifetime caps?
- How often does the rate adjust after the fixed period?
- What would my payment be at the maximum possible rate?
- Is there a prepayment penalty if I sell or refinance early?

## Putting It Together

A fixed-rate mortgage buys certainty: the principal-and-interest payment you
sign for is the one you'll pay for decades. An ARM buys a lower starting
rate, but after the fixed period your rate follows the index plus the
margin, limited only by the caps. Choose an ARM when you have a clear reason
to expect you won't hold the loan past the fixed period and you could handle
the worst-case payment; otherwise, the predictability of a fixed rate is
usually worth the premium.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a fixed or adjustable rate better right now?** It depends less on the
headline rates than on how long you'll keep the loan. Compare the ARM's
savings during its fixed period against its worst-case payment afterward. If
you'd keep the loan well past the reset, the fixed rate's certainty is
usually worth paying for.

**Is it harder to qualify for an ARM?** It can be. Lenders often qualify ARM
borrowers at a rate above the introductory rate, to make sure the payment is
still affordable after it adjusts, so the same income may support a smaller
ARM than you'd expect from the starting payment alone.

**Can I switch from an ARM to a fixed rate later?** Usually by refinancing,
which means qualifying again and paying closing costs. Refinancing is never
guaranteed — if rates have risen or your finances have changed, the fixed
rate available to you may be higher than the one you passed up.

**Does a fixed-rate mortgage payment ever change?** The principal and
interest don't. If your taxes and insurance are paid through an escrow
account, though, your total monthly payment can still rise when those costs
go up.
