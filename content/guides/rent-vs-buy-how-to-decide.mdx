"Renting is throwing money away" is one of the most repeated lines in personal
finance, and it's only half true. Owners throw money away too — on mortgage
interest, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and the steep costs of
buying and selling. The real question isn't whether renting or owning costs
money. It's which one costs *less* over the period you expect to stay, once
every cost on both sides is counted. This guide walks through the pieces that
matter and how to weigh them.

## Compare Unrecoverable Costs, Not Monthly Payments

The most common mistake is comparing a rent check to a mortgage payment. They
aren't equivalent. Part of each mortgage payment goes to principal, which
builds equity you get back when you sell. Rent builds nothing, but it also
comes with no repair bills, no property tax, and no tied-up down payment.

A fairer comparison sets aside the parts of each that you get back and focuses
on the costs that are simply gone:

**For a renter**, the unrecoverable cost is mostly the rent itself, plus
renters insurance.

**For an owner**, the unrecoverable costs are:

- **Mortgage interest** — not principal, which comes back as equity.
- **Property taxes**, which vary widely by state and locality.
- **Homeowners insurance** and any HOA dues.
- **Maintenance and repairs.** A common planning rule is to budget around 1%
  of the home's value per year, though older homes and harsh climates can run
  higher.
- **Opportunity cost** of the money tied up in the home (covered below).
- **Transaction costs** to buy and eventually sell.

### The 5% Rule of Thumb

One widely used shortcut bundles the ongoing owner costs into a single
estimate: roughly 1% of the home's value for property tax, 1% for maintenance,
and around 3% for the cost of capital (mortgage interest on the borrowed
portion and the forgone return on your down payment). Together that's about
**5% of the home's value per year**.

Divide by 12 and compare the result to the rent on an equivalent home:

```
Break-even monthly rent ≈ Home price × 5% ÷ 12
```

For a $400,000 home, that's $400,000 × 5% = $20,000 a year, or about **$1,667
a month**. If a comparable home rents for well under that, renting is probably
the cheaper option; if it rents for well over, buying likely wins over time.

Treat the 5% as a starting point, not a law. In a high-tax area, with a higher
mortgage rate, or with an older house, the true figure may be 6% or more. In a
low-tax area with a low rate, it could be closer to 4%. Swap in your own
numbers whenever you can.

## The Opportunity Cost of a Down Payment

A down payment isn't a cost in the sense that it disappears — it becomes
equity. But money in your home's walls is money that isn't invested elsewhere,
and that has a cost.

Suppose you put 20% down on a $400,000 home: $80,000. If that money had
instead stayed invested and earned, say, 5% a year, it would have generated
about $80,000 × 5% = **$4,000 a year**. That's real money you give up by
buying, even though it never appears on a bill.

The rate you choose matters. A conservative assumption (a high-yield savings
account) makes buying look better; an aggressive one (long-run stock market
returns) makes renting look better. Either way, a fair comparison includes it
— and the home's own appreciation is what has to offset it.

## Transaction Costs and the Break-Even Horizon

Buying and selling a home is expensive. Buyers typically pay closing costs —
lender fees, title insurance, appraisal, and prepaid items — commonly
estimated at around 2% to 5% of the loan amount. Sellers usually pay agent
commissions plus transfer taxes and other fees, which together often add up to
several percent of the sale price more.

These are one-time costs, so the longer you stay, the more years you have to
spread them across. That creates a **break-even horizon**: the number of years
you need to stay before owning comes out ahead of renting.

### A Simplified Example

Take the same $400,000 home, and suppose a comparable rental costs $2,000 a
month, or $24,000 a year.

- Annual owner cost (5% rule): $20,000
- Annual renter cost: $24,000
- Annual advantage of owning: $24,000 − $20,000 = **$4,000**

Now the one-time costs, assuming 3% to buy and 8% to sell:

- Buying costs: $400,000 × 3% = $12,000
- Selling costs: $400,000 × 8% = $32,000
- Total transaction costs: **$44,000**

The break-even horizon is roughly:

```
$44,000 ÷ $4,000 per year = 11 years
```

In this example, if you expect to move within about a decade, renting likely
leaves you better off. Stay longer and owning pulls ahead.

This is a deliberate simplification. Home price appreciation would shorten the
horizon (the home is worth more when you sell), and rising rents would shorten
it further. A flat or falling market, a large unexpected repair, or a
higher-than-planned mortgage rate would lengthen it. The [rent vs. buy
calculator](/calculators/rent-vs-buy-calculator) models these factors year by
year so you can see how sensitive the answer is to each one.

## The Price-to-Rent Ratio

To judge whether a whole market leans toward buying or renting, analysts often
use the price-to-rent ratio:

```
Price-to-rent ratio = Home price ÷ Annual rent for a comparable home
```

For the example above: $400,000 ÷ $24,000 = **16.7**.

A commonly cited rough guide reads the ratio like this:

- **Below about 15:** buying tends to be favorable relative to renting.
- **About 15 to 20:** a gray zone where the details of your situation decide
  it.
- **Above about 20:** renting tends to be favorable.

These cutoffs aren't precise, and they shift with interest rates — when
mortgage rates are high, the threshold for buying to make sense effectively
drops. Use the ratio to understand your local market, then do the detailed
comparison for the specific home you're considering.

## What Owning Gives You That a Spreadsheet Doesn't

Even a perfect financial comparison leaves out things that matter.

**Reasons owning may be worth more than the numbers suggest:**

- **Stability.** No landlord can decide not to renew your lease or sell the
  building out from under you.
- **Control.** You can renovate, paint, garden, or get a dog without asking
  permission.
- **Forced savings.** Principal payments build equity automatically, which
  helps people who wouldn't otherwise invest the difference.
- **Predictable housing cost.** With a fixed-rate mortgage, principal and
  interest don't rise with the rental market — though taxes and insurance can.

**Reasons renting may be worth more than the numbers suggest:**

- **Flexibility.** If your job, relationship, or city might change in the next
  few years, the ability to leave with a month or two of notice is valuable.
- **No surprise repairs.** A failed roof or furnace is the landlord's problem.
- **Liquidity and diversification.** Instead of concentrating your net worth
  in one property in one neighborhood, you can keep savings spread across
  investments you can access quickly.
- **Less time and effort.** Owning comes with ongoing maintenance and
  decisions that some people would rather not handle.

## Questions to Ask Yourself

Before deciding, work through these honestly:

1. **How long will I stay?** If the answer is under five years, buying is hard
   to justify financially in most markets because transaction costs dominate.
2. **Can I afford to own, not just to buy?** A down payment is only the first
   hurdle. You'll need cash reserves for closing costs, moving, and the
   repairs that inevitably come.
3. **Is my income stable?** A mortgage is a long-term commitment that's harder
   to walk away from than a lease.
4. **Would I actually invest the difference if I rent?** Renting wins in many
   comparisons only if the money saved goes into investments rather than
   everyday spending.
5. **What does the local price-to-rent ratio say?** In some markets, renting
   is far cheaper; in others, owning is.

## Putting It Together

Compare the unrecoverable costs of each option rather than rent against a
mortgage payment. Estimate the owner's annual costs — interest, taxes,
insurance, maintenance, and the opportunity cost of the down payment — and
compare them to the rent on an equivalent home. Then factor in transaction
costs to find how many years you'd need to stay for buying to pay off, and
weigh that against how long you realistically expect to live there.
