If you have several debts and want to pay them off faster than the minimums
allow, you need a plan for where the extra money goes. The two most popular
plans are the **debt snowball** and the **debt avalanche**. Both work, and
both are far better than spreading extra payments randomly. They differ in one
decision — which debt to attack first — and that single choice trades a little
money for a lot of motivation, or the reverse. This guide explains how each
method works, runs both on the same set of debts, and helps you choose.

## What the Two Methods Have in Common

Both strategies follow the same basic structure:

1. **List every debt** with its balance, interest rate, and minimum payment.
2. **Decide on a fixed monthly amount** you'll put toward debt — more than the
   sum of your minimums.
3. **Pay the minimum on every debt** every month, so nothing falls behind.
4. **Send all the extra money to one target debt** until it's paid off.
5. **Roll that debt's payment into the next target.** When one debt
   disappears, its minimum payment doesn't go back into your budget — it joins
   the extra money aimed at the next debt on the list.

That last step is what gives both methods their power. The amount going to
your current target grows each time you knock out a debt, so payoff speeds up
as you go.

The only difference is the order of the list.

## The Debt Snowball: Smallest Balance First

With the snowball method, you order your debts from **smallest balance to
largest**, regardless of interest rate. You throw your extra money at the
smallest one first.

The appeal is psychological. Small balances disappear quickly, giving you
early, visible wins. Each paid-off account is one less bill, one less
statement, and proof that the plan is working. The approach was popularized
largely on the argument that for many people, paying off debt is more a
behavior problem than a math problem — and that the motivation from quick wins
keeps people going long enough to finish.

## The Debt Avalanche: Highest Interest Rate First

With the avalanche method, you order your debts from **highest interest rate
to lowest**, regardless of balance. Extra money goes to the most expensive
debt first.

The appeal is mathematical. Interest is the cost of carrying debt, and every
extra dollar sent to your highest-rate balance eliminates the most expensive
interest first. Given the same monthly budget, paying debts in this order
results in **the least total interest** of any order, and it typically gets
you debt-free as fast or faster.

The downside is that your highest-rate debt might also be your largest, and it
could take many months before you pay off a single account.

## A Worked Example

Suppose you have three debts and can put **$600 a month** toward them in
total:

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum payment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Store card | $1,200 | 15% | $40 |
| Credit card | $6,000 | 24% | $180 |
| Car loan | $4,500 | 6% | $150 |
| **Total** | **$11,700** | | **$370** |

The minimums add up to $370, so you have $600 − $370 = **$230 a month** in
extra money to direct at a target.

For simplicity, this example assumes interest accrues monthly at one-twelfth
of the APR, minimum payments stay fixed, and you don't add any new debt.

**Snowball order (smallest balance first):** store card ($1,200), car loan
($4,500), credit card ($6,000).

**Avalanche order (highest rate first):** credit card (24%), store card (15%),
car loan (6%).

### Snowball Results

- **Month 5:** The store card is paid off. Its $40 minimum rolls into the car
  loan payment.
- **Month 15:** The car loan is paid off. Its payment rolls into the credit
  card.
- **Month 24:** The credit card is paid off. You're debt-free.
- **Total interest paid: about $2,353**

### Avalanche Results

- **Month 18:** The credit card is paid off. Its $180 minimum rolls into the
  store card.
- **Month 20:** The store card is paid off.
- **Month 23:** The car loan is paid off. You're debt-free.
- **Total interest paid: about $1,734**

### Comparing the Two

| | Snowball | Avalanche |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First debt paid off | Month 5 | Month 18 |
| Debt-free | Month 24 | Month 23 |
| Total interest | ≈ $2,353 | ≈ $1,734 |

The avalanche saves about **$620 in interest** and finishes one month sooner.
That's the mathematical case: the snowball spent over a year aiming extra
money at a 6% car loan while a 24% credit card balance kept accruing interest
in the background.

But look at the first row. With the snowball, you have your first paid-off
account in five months and your second by month 15. With the avalanche, you
make payments for a year and a half before a single account reaches zero. If
you're the kind of person who might lose steam during those 18 months, the
snowball's early wins could be worth far more than $620 — because the most
expensive debt plan is the one you abandon partway through.

### Why the Results Differ by This Much

How much the avalanche saves depends on how your debts are arranged. The gap
is largest when your **biggest balance also carries the highest rate**, as in
this example, because the snowball leaves the most expensive debt for last.
When your smallest balances happen to have the highest rates, the two methods
produce the same order and identical results. Before choosing, it's worth
running your own debts through both orders to see how big the difference
actually is.

## Which Method Should You Choose?

**The avalanche is usually the better fit if:**

- You're motivated by numbers and can stay committed without quick wins.
- Your interest rates are spread far apart, so the savings are large.
- Your highest-rate debt is a large balance that would otherwise sit untouched
  for a long time under the snowball.

**The snowball is usually the better fit if:**

- You've tried to pay off debt before and lost momentum.
- You have several small balances you could eliminate quickly.
- Your interest rates are fairly similar, so the snowball costs little extra.
- Reducing the number of bills and due dates would make your finances easier
  to manage.

Neither method matters if the plan doesn't stick. The biggest gains come from
the decision to pay more than the minimums every month and to stop adding new
debt — far bigger than the difference between the two orders.

## Hybrid Approaches

You don't have to pick one method in its purest form. Common middle grounds
include:

- **Quick win, then avalanche.** If you have one very small balance, pay it
  off first for the motivation, then switch to highest-rate-first for
  everything else. The cost of a single small detour is usually modest.
- **Avalanche with a tiebreaker.** When two debts have similar rates, target
  the smaller balance first. You give up very little interest and get a faster
  win.
- **Snowball with an exception.** Follow the snowball order but move any
  unusually high-rate debt — like a credit card with a penalty APR — to the
  front of the line.
- **Switch partway through.** Start with the snowball to build momentum, then
  move to the avalanche once your remaining debts are larger and longer-term.

## Ways to Speed Up Either Plan

- **Lower your rates.** A balance transfer to a card with a low introductory
  rate, or a lower-rate consolidation loan, can cut interest costs
  significantly — as long as you understand the transfer fees, when the
  promotional rate ends, and don't run the old card balances back up.
- **Direct windfalls at your target debt.** Tax refunds, bonuses, and raises
  applied to debt can shorten the timeline by months.
- **Keep a small emergency fund.** Without one, an unexpected car repair goes
  straight onto a credit card and undoes your progress.
- **Stop adding new debt.** Neither strategy works if balances keep growing
  behind you.

## Putting It Together

Both methods share the same engine: pay every minimum, send all extra money to
a single target, and roll each paid-off payment into the next debt. The
avalanche targets the highest interest rate and usually costs the least; the
snowball targets the smallest balance and delivers wins sooner. Pick the one
you're most likely to follow through on, or combine them.
