Look at the first payment on a 30-year mortgage and you might be surprised how
little of it goes toward the loan balance. That's not a mistake or a hidden
fee — it's how amortization works, and understanding it explains a lot about
mortgages that otherwise seems counterintuitive. This guide shows how the
payment is calculated, follows a real loan from the first payment to the
last, and explains what amortization means for equity, mortgage insurance,
and refinancing.

## The Core Mechanic: Interest on the Current Balance

Every month, interest is calculated on whatever balance remains — not on the
original loan amount. Early in the loan, the balance is at its highest point,
so the interest charge is also at its highest point. Because your monthly
payment is fixed, whatever's left after covering that interest is what goes
toward principal. High balance means high interest means a small principal
portion, especially in the loan's first years.

## How the Monthly Payment Is Calculated

A fixed-rate mortgage payment is set so that, if every payment is made on
schedule, the balance reaches exactly zero with the final payment. The
standard formula is:

```
Payment = P × r × (1 + r) ^ n ÷ ((1 + r) ^ n − 1)
```

- **P** is the amount borrowed.
- **r** is the monthly interest rate — the annual rate divided by 12.
- **n** is the total number of monthly payments (360 for a 30-year loan).

For a $320,000 loan at an example rate of 6.5%, r is 0.065 ÷ 12, or about
0.005417, and n is 360. The formula gives a monthly principal-and-interest
payment of **$2,022.62**. Each month's split is then simple arithmetic:

```
Interest this month  = remaining balance × r
Principal this month = payment − interest this month
New balance          = remaining balance − principal this month
```

This is the whole engine of amortization.

## Why the Split Shifts Over Time

As principal is paid down — even slowly at first — the balance shrinks, which
shrinks next month's interest charge, which leaves a slightly larger share of
the fixed payment for principal. This creates a feedback loop: more principal
paid down leads to less interest charged, which leads to even more principal
paid down the following month. The shift is slow at the start and dramatic
by the end.

## A Concrete Illustration

On a $320,000 loan at 6.5% over 30 years, the very first payment of roughly
$2,023 includes about $1,733 in interest and only $290 in principal — interest
makes up over 85% of that first payment. By the final year of the loan, that
ratio has essentially flipped, with the overwhelming majority of each payment
reducing principal.

Here are the first three payments:

| Payment | Interest | Principal | Remaining balance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $1,733.33 | $289.28 | $319,710.72 |
| 2 | $1,731.77 | $290.85 | $319,419.86 |
| 3 | $1,730.19 | $292.43 | $319,127.44 |

Principal grows by only about $1.50 a month at first. Zooming out to whole
years shows how the pace picks up:

| Year | Interest paid that year | Principal paid that year | Balance at year end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $20,695 | $3,577 | $316,423 |
| 5 | $19,636 | $4,636 | $299,555 |
| 10 | $17,861 | $6,410 | $271,284 |
| 15 | $15,407 | $8,864 | $232,189 |
| 20 | $12,014 | $12,257 | $178,129 |
| 25 | $7,322 | $16,949 | $103,373 |
| 30 | $833 | $23,438 | $0 |

A few milestones on this loan are worth noticing:

- **After 5 years** you've paid about $121,357, but only $20,445 of it has
  reduced the balance; the other $100,912 was interest.
- **After 10 years** you've paid down $48,716 — about 15% of the loan.
- **The crossover** — the first month in which principal exceeds interest —
  doesn't arrive until payment 233, in the 20th year.
- **Half the balance** is paid off only at payment 257, about 21 years and 5
  months in.
- **Total interest** over the full 30 years comes to about $408,142, more
  than the amount borrowed.

The [15 vs. 30-year mortgage](/guides/15-vs-30-year-mortgage) guide shows how
much faster this curve moves on a shorter term.

## What Amortization Means for Your Equity

Your home equity is the home's value minus what you owe. Amortization is one
of two ways it grows — the other is any change in the home's value, which
isn't guaranteed. Because principal paydown is slow early on, a buyer who
sells after a few years usually has built relatively little equity through
payments alone, and selling costs can consume much of it.

Amortization also governs when private mortgage insurance (PMI) can end on a
conventional loan. Under the Homeowners Protection Act, you can ask the
servicer to cancel PMI once the balance falls to 80% of the home's original
value, and it terminates automatically at 78% if you're current on payments.
Suppose you bought a $400,000 home with a $360,000 loan at the same example
6.5% rate (a payment of $2,275.44). Making only the scheduled payments:

- The balance reaches 80% of the original value ($320,000) at **payment 95**,
  about 7 years and 11 months in.
- It reaches 78% ($312,000) at **payment 109**, about 9 years and 1 month
  in.

Extra principal payments pull both dates closer. The guide to [how extra
mortgage payments save interest](/guides/how-extra-mortgage-payments-save-interest)
covers that strategy in detail.

## Refinancing Restarts the Clock

When you refinance, the remaining balance is amortized again from scratch over
the new term. That can lower the payment, but it also puts you back at the
interest-heavy start of a new schedule.

Take the $320,000 loan above after 5 years, with $299,555 still owed and
$307,230 of interest left to pay on the original schedule. Suppose you could
refinance at an example rate of 5.5% (closing costs not included):

| Option | Monthly payment | Interest from here on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep original loan (25 years left) | $2,022.62 | $307,230 |
| Refinance into a new 30-year | $1,700.84 | $312,748 |
| Refinance into a new 25-year | $1,839.53 | $252,304 |

The new 30-year loan cuts the payment by about $322 a month, yet you'd pay
slightly *more* total interest than if you'd kept the original loan, because
the balance is stretched over five extra years. Matching the remaining term
keeps most of the rate benefit — about $54,926 less interest before closing
costs. Neither choice is wrong, but compare total cost as well as the payment.
The [refinance calculator](/calculators/refinance-calculator) runs this
comparison with your closing costs included.

## Amortization on Other Loans

Auto loans and personal installment loans amortize the same way, but shorter
terms make the pattern much less lopsided. A $30,000 auto loan at an example
7% rate over 5 years has a payment of $594.04. The first payment is $175.00
interest and $419.04 principal — principal exceeds interest from the very
first month — and total interest over the loan is about $5,642. The [loan
calculator](/calculators/loan-calculator) and [auto loan
calculator](/calculators/auto-loan-calculator) show the full schedules.

Not every loan fully amortizes. With an **interest-only** loan, early
payments cover only interest and the balance doesn't fall. With **negative
amortization**, payments are smaller than the interest due, and the unpaid
interest is added to the balance, so you owe more over time. If a loan offer
mentions either feature, read the terms closely.

## Common Misconceptions

**"The bank charges more interest at the start."** The rate is constant. More
interest is paid early only because more is owed early.

**"My payment includes taxes and insurance, so they're amortizing too."**
Many payments include escrow amounts for property taxes and homeowners
insurance, but those aren't part of the amortization schedule. They can rise
or fall from year to year even when your principal and interest stay fixed.

## Why Extra Payments Are So Effective Early On

Because so little of an early payment reduces principal, any extra amount you
add goes entirely toward principal — and that extra principal stops accruing
interest immediately, for every remaining month of the loan. This is why
extra payments made early in a mortgage save disproportionately more interest
than the same extra payment made later, when the balance and its accompanying
interest charge are already much lower.

## Reading Your Own Amortization Schedule

An amortization schedule lists every payment with its principal, interest,
and remaining balance — exactly what the [mortgage
calculator](/calculators/mortgage-calculator) generates for any loan amount,
rate, and term you enter, including the effect of any extra monthly payment.
The [loan calculator](/calculators/loan-calculator) produces the same
breakdown for non-mortgage instalment loans.

When you read one, look for the year when principal overtakes interest, the
balance at the point you might sell or refinance, and the total interest over
the life of the loan. Those three numbers say more about a loan than the
monthly payment alone.

## Putting It Together

Amortization is simple arithmetic repeated hundreds of times: interest on the
current balance, the rest of the fixed payment to principal. On a long
mortgage, that makes the early years overwhelmingly interest and the later
years overwhelmingly principal. Knowing where you are on that curve helps with
real decisions — how much equity you'll have if you sell, when PMI can end,
whether a refinance actually saves money, and how much an extra payment is
worth today.
