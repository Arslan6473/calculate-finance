Compound interest is often called the most powerful force in personal
finance, and the reason is simple: once interest is added to your balance, it
starts earning interest of its own. Over enough time, that second layer of
growth — interest on interest — can end up contributing more to your final
balance than everything you put in.

## How to Use This Compound Interest Calculator

Enter your starting deposit and the annual interest rate you expect to earn,
then choose how often interest compounds — daily, monthly, quarterly,
semiannually, or annually. Set the number of years you plan to leave the
money in place. If you plan to add money regularly, enter the contribution
amount and how often you'll make it (monthly, quarterly, or annually); the
calculator handles a different compounding and contribution schedule at the
same time — for example, a monthly deposit into an account that compounds
quarterly. The results show your final balance, how much of it you
contributed, how much is interest, the effective annual rate, and a
year-by-year breakdown.

## How Compound Interest Is Calculated

Each time interest compounds, it's calculated on the current balance —
including any interest already added in previous periods — and then added to
that balance. The next compounding period earns interest on this new, larger
total. This calculator steps through your chosen term period by period,
applying interest and contributions in order, rather than using a single
formula that would have to approximate irregular schedules. Contributions are
treated as deposits made at the end of each contribution period, so a deposit
starts earning interest from the following compounding period.

## Compound Interest Formula

For compounding with no contributions, the balance after **t** years is:

```
A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)
```

- **A** is the ending balance.
- **P** is the principal — your opening deposit.
- **r** is the annual interest rate as a decimal (6% is 0.06).
- **n** is the number of times interest compounds per year: 1 for annually,
  4 for quarterly, 12 for monthly, 365 for daily.
- **t** is the number of years.

The calculator also reports the effective annual rate, which converts any
compounding frequency into a single comparable yearly figure:

```
Effective annual rate = (1 + r/n)^n − 1
```

When contributions are added, the calculator uses the step-by-step method
described above rather than a closed-form extension of the formula, since it
needs to support contribution schedules that don't line up with the
compounding schedule.

## Example Calculation

$5,000 deposited at 6% annual interest, compounded monthly, with no further
contributions, grows to $16,551.02 after 20 years — more than three times the
original deposit, with $11,551.02 of that being interest. The effective
annual rate is 6.17%, slightly above the stated 6% because interest is added
twelve times a year.

The compounding frequency matters, but less than people expect. The same
$5,000 at 6% for 20 years ends at $16,035.68 with annual compounding and
$16,598.95 with daily compounding — a difference of about $563 over two
decades.

Contributions matter far more. Add $200 a month to the original monthly
compounding scenario and the ending balance reaches $108,959.20. You'd have
deposited $53,000 in total, and the remaining $55,959.20 is interest. Cut the
same plan to 10 years and the balance is $41,872.85, of which only $12,872.85
is interest — the second decade produces most of the growth.

## What Affects Compound Growth

- **Time** — the single biggest driver. Compounding needs time to build
  momentum; the same deposit left in twice as long can produce far more than
  double the growth.
- **Interest rate** — a higher rate compounds faster, and the effect
  amplifies over long periods.
- **Compounding frequency** — more frequent compounding (daily versus
  annual) produces a modestly higher return at the same stated rate, since
  interest starts earning its own interest sooner.
- **Contributions** — regular deposits, even modest ones, often add more to
  your final balance over a long horizon than the interest rate itself.
- **Withdrawals** — taking money out resets part of the compounding base;
  interest you withdraw can no longer earn interest of its own.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Comparing accounts by stated rate when compounding differs.** A rate
  compounded daily and the same rate compounded annually aren't equal. Use
  the effective annual rate to compare them on the same footing.
- **Assuming a savings rate will hold for decades.** Rates on savings
  accounts and CDs change over time. A 20-year projection at today's rate is
  an illustration, not a forecast; try a lower rate to see a cautious case.
- **Overestimating the frequency effect.** Moving from annual to daily
  compounding added about $563 in the example. Adding to your deposits or
  extending the term usually matters far more.
- **Ignoring taxes on interest.** Interest on bank accounts and CDs is
  generally taxable income, which reduces what's left to compound in a
  taxable account. This calculator shows pre-tax growth.
- **Forgetting inflation.** A balance that triples in nominal terms buys less
  than three times as much if prices rise over the same period.
- **Starting later to save a little more.** The 10-year versus 20-year
  example shows why: most of the interest arrives in the later years, and
  those years can't be recovered by contributing more at the end.
