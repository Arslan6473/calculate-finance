Return on investment sounds simple — profit divided by cost — but two details
change the answer more than people expect: whether you've counted every real
cost, and whether you've compared returns over the same length of time. This
calculator handles both.

## How to Use This ROI Calculator

Enter what you initially invested and the final value of that investment. If
you paid fees, commissions, or other costs alongside the investment, add them
under "additional costs" — they're part of what you actually spent. If the
investment paid you income along the way, such as dividends or rent, add that
under "income received." Enter the holding period in months to also see your
return expressed as an annualized rate, which is what makes returns over
different time periods comparable. The results show your ROI, net profit,
annualized ROI, total invested, and total returned.

## How ROI Is Calculated

```
ROI = (Total Returned − Total Invested) / Total Invested × 100%
```

- **Total Invested** is your initial investment plus any additional costs —
  fees, commissions, maintenance, or anything else you paid to own the
  investment.
- **Total Returned** is the final value plus any income received during the
  holding period, such as dividends, rent, or interest.
- **Total Returned − Total Invested** is your net profit, which is negative
  on a loss.

Leaving out fees or income is the most common way ROI calculations mislead —
a $10,000 investment with $500 in fees has $10,500 truly at risk, not
$10,000.

## Why Annualized ROI Isn't Just ROI Divided by Time

A 100% return over two years is not a 50% annual return — it's about 41.4%.
The difference is compounding: growing by 41.4% twice in a row multiplies your
money by 2×, not by adding 41.4% + 41.4%. This calculator uses the
compounding formula:

```
Annualized ROI = (Total Returned / Total Invested)^(1 / years) − 1
```

Here **years** is the holding period in months divided by 12. The ratio
inside the brackets is the multiple of your money you got back; raising it to
the power of one over the number of years finds the constant yearly growth
rate that would produce that same multiple.

When you enter a holding period and the investment is profitable, the
calculator also estimates a break-even time: how many months your average
monthly profit would take to add up to the full amount you invested.

## Example Calculation

$10,000 invested and grown to $15,000 over 24 months, with no additional
costs or income, has an ROI of 50%. Annualized, that's 22.47% per year — the
constant yearly rate that compounds to the same 50% total gain over two
years.

Now add the real-world details: $500 in purchase commissions and fees, and
$300 in dividends received along the way. Total invested becomes $10,500,
total returned becomes $15,300, and net profit is $4,800. ROI falls to 45.71%,
and the annualized return to 20.71%.

The holding period changes the picture far more than the fees did. The same
45.71% gain earned over 60 months annualizes to just 7.82% a year. Earned
over 6 months, it annualizes to 112.33% — a figure that looks impressive but
assumes you could repeat that performance every half-year, which is rarely
realistic.

Losses work the same way in reverse: $10,000 that falls to $8,000 over 24
months is an ROI of −20%, or −10.56% annualized.

## What Affects Your ROI

- **Additional costs** — fees and commissions raise your true denominator
  and lower your real ROI, even though the sticker price of the investment
  didn't change.
- **Income received** — dividends, rent, or interest add to your return even
  if the asset's value alone didn't grow much.
- **Holding period** — the same total ROI is a very different result
  annualized over one year versus ten.
- **Exit costs** — selling often carries its own commissions or closing
  costs. Subtract them from the final value, or add them to costs, so the
  return reflects what you actually keep.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Comparing total ROI across different time periods.** A 45% gain over five
  years and a 30% gain over two years can't be ranked by the headline number.
  Compare annualized ROI instead.
- **Dividing total ROI by the number of years.** That ignores compounding and
  overstates the yearly return on multi-year gains. A 100% two-year return
  is 41.42% a year, not 50%.
- **Leaving out the costs of owning the investment.** Purchase fees,
  advisory fees, repairs on a property, and platform charges all belong in
  additional costs. Omitting them flatters the result.
- **Forgetting income you received.** Reinvested dividends or rent you spent
  are still part of the return. Leave them out and you understate ROI.
- **Annualizing very short holding periods.** Annualizing a few months of
  gains assumes the same pace continues all year. Treat those figures as
  descriptive, not as an expected rate.
- **Ignoring taxes.** ROI here is pre-tax. Gains on assets held more than a
  year are generally taxed differently from gains on assets held a year or
  less, so the same pre-tax ROI can leave you with different after-tax
  amounts.
