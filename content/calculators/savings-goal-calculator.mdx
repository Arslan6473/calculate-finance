Most savings goals start as a number and a date: a $20,000 down payment in
four years, a $10,000 emergency fund by next summer, a $50,000 college fund
by the time a child turns eighteen. The hard part is turning that into a
plan you can follow every month. This calculator works backward from the
goal to the one figure that matters day to day — how much to set aside each
month — and shows how much of the finished balance comes from your deposits
versus interest.

## How to Use This Savings Goal Calculator

Enter the amount you want to have and what you have already saved toward
it. Then set the number of years until you need the money and the annual
interest or return rate you expect. For a high-yield savings account or CD,
use its APY; for an investment account, use a conservative estimate; for
plain cash, enter 0%.

Choose how often interest compounds — monthly is typical for savings
accounts — and whether you will deposit at the start or the end of each
month. The results show the required monthly deposit, your total
contributions, the interest you can expect to earn, and a year-by-year chart
of your progress. If your current savings are already enough, or will grow
to the goal on their own, the calculator tells you so instead of asking for
deposits you do not need.

## How the Required Deposit Is Calculated

The calculator first grows your current savings forward to the goal date.
Whatever is still missing — the shortfall — has to come from monthly
deposits. It then divides that shortfall by the future value of depositing
one dollar a month at your rate, which gives the exact deposit that closes
the gap on time.

## Savings Goal Formula

```
Shortfall = Goal − PV × (1 + i)^N

PMT = Shortfall ÷ [ ((1 + i)^N − 1) ÷ i ]          (end-of-month deposits)
PMT = Shortfall ÷ [ ((1 + i)^N − 1) ÷ i × (1 + i) ]  (start-of-month deposits)
```

**PV** is your current savings, **N** is the number of months, and **i** is
the monthly rate. With monthly compounding, i is simply the annual rate
divided by 12. For any other compounding frequency, the calculator converts
to the equivalent monthly rate, (1 + r/n)^(n/12) − 1, so every option is
handled exactly. At a 0% rate the formula reduces to the shortfall divided
evenly across the months.

## Example Calculation

Suppose your goal is $50,000 in 10 years, you already have $5,000 saved,
and you expect to earn 5% a year, compounded monthly, with deposits at the
end of each month.

- **Monthly rate**: 5% ÷ 12 = 0.4167%, over 120 months.
- **Current savings at the goal date**: $5,000 × (1.004167)^120 = $8,235.05.
- **Shortfall**: $50,000 − $8,235.05 = $41,764.95.
- **Annuity factor**: ((1.004167)^120 − 1) ÷ 0.004167 ≈ 155.28.
- **Required deposit**: $41,764.95 ÷ 155.28 ≈ **$268.96 a month**.

Over the 10 years you contribute $37,275.38 in total — the $5,000 you
started with plus about $32,275 in monthly deposits — and interest supplies
the remaining $12,724.62. Moving the deposits to the start of each month
lowers the requirement slightly, to $267.85. With no interest at all, you
would need $375 a month to cover the same $45,000 gap.

## What Affects How Much You Need to Save

- **Time** — the more months you have, the smaller each deposit and the
  more of the goal interest can cover. Starting even a year or two earlier
  makes a noticeable difference.
- **Your starting balance** — money already saved gets the longest time to
  compound, so it reduces the required deposit by more than its face value.
- **The rate** — a higher rate lowers the deposit, but only if it is a rate
  you can actually earn. For short goals, the safety of the money usually
  matters more than squeezing out extra return.
- **Deposit timing** — depositing at the start of the month gives each
  deposit one more month of growth. The effect is small but free.
- **Inflation** — a goal set in today's dollars will cost more by the time
  you reach it. Consider raising a long-term target to allow for rising
  prices.

## Where to Keep Money for a Savings Goal

For goals a few years away, many savers use insured savings accounts,
money market deposit accounts, or CDs. Deposits at FDIC-insured banks are
protected up to the standard insurance limit per depositor, per bank, per
ownership category, so the balance cannot fall because of a market drop.
Longer goals can tolerate investments with higher expected returns and more
risk, but the closer you get to the date, the less time there is to recover
from a downturn.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Using an optimistic rate** — planning around a best-case return makes
  the required deposit look smaller than it really is. Use a rate you are
  confident of, and recalculate if it changes.
- **Ignoring inflation on long goals** — $50,000 in ten years will not buy
  what $50,000 buys today. Inflate the target if it is tied to a real cost.
- **Investing short-term money in volatile assets** — a market decline in
  the final year can leave you short just when you need the money.
- **Forgetting taxes on interest** — interest in a regular savings account
  is generally taxable, which slightly reduces your effective rate.
- **Skipping months** — the plan assumes every deposit is made. Automating
  a transfer on payday is the easiest way to stay on schedule.
