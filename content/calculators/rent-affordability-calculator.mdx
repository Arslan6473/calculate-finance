There is no single correct answer to "how much rent can I afford?" — there
are several rules of thumb, each looking at the question from a different
angle. This calculator works out four of them side by side and recommends the
lowest, so you can see where they agree, where they differ, and why.

## How to Use This Rent Affordability Calculator

Enter your gross income — before tax — and choose whether it is per year or
per month. Add the minimum monthly payments on any debts, such as a car loan,
student loans or credit cards. Set your take-home pay as a share of gross
(75% is a reasonable starting point for many earners; check a recent payslip
for yours), and optionally add other essentials such as groceries, utilities,
transport and insurance. Finally, set the share of gross income you are
willing to put toward rent. It defaults to 30%.

## The Four Rules

**The 30% rule.** Spend no more than 30% of gross monthly income on rent. It
comes from US housing policy and is the most widely quoted guideline, but it
ignores debts, taxes and local costs. You can change the percentage.

**The 50/30/20 needs budget.** Split take-home pay into 50% for needs, 30%
for wants and 20% for savings and extra debt payments. Rent is a need, so it
has to share the 50% bucket with minimum debt payments and other essentials.
This calculator takes half your take-home pay, subtracts those, and treats
the rest as the most rent the needs bucket can hold.

**The landlord 40× rule.** Many landlords require annual gross income of at
least 40 times the monthly rent, so the maximum is annual income ÷ 40. This
is not a budgeting rule — it is what you will be screened against when you
apply. Because 12 ÷ 40 = 0.3, it works out to exactly 30% of gross monthly
income.

**The 36% debt-to-income view.** Borrowed from mortgage lending, this caps
rent plus all other debt payments at 36% of gross monthly income. It is the
rule that most directly penalises existing debt.

```
30% rule       = gross monthly income × 30%
50/30/20 needs = take-home monthly × 50% − debts − other essentials
40× rule       = annual gross income ÷ 40
36% DTI        = gross monthly income × 36% − debts
```

## Example Calculation

Someone earning $60,000 a year has a gross monthly income of $5,000. With
take-home pay at 75%, that is $3,750 a month. They pay $400 a month toward a
car loan and spend about $500 a month on groceries, utilities and transport.

| Rule | Maximum rent |
| --- | --- |
| 30% of gross income | $1,500 |
| Landlord 40× rule ($60,000 ÷ 40) | $1,500 |
| 36% DTI ($1,800 − $400) | $1,400 |
| 50/30/20 needs ($1,875 − $400 − $500) | $975 |

The recommended figure is the lowest, **$975**, set by the needs budget.
That is noticeably below the familiar $1,500 headline, because the 30% and
40× rules ignore the car payment and everyday essentials entirely.

If the same person leaves other essentials at zero, the needs budget rises to
$1,475 and the 36% rule becomes the limit at **$1,400** — still $100 below
what the 30% rule suggests, because of the car loan.

## Why These Are Only Rules of Thumb

Each rule is a simplification. The 30% guideline is too tight for some
low-cost situations and too loose for someone with high debt or childcare
costs. The 40× rule is a screening test, not advice about what is
comfortable. Take-home pay varies with tax, retirement contributions and
benefits. A budget built from your actual spending is always a better guide
than any rule, and in expensive cities many renters knowingly spend more than
30% while cutting elsewhere. That is why housing researchers often look at
residual income — what is left after rent — alongside the 30% share. The
50/30/20 figure here is a simple version of that idea.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Applying the 30% rule to take-home pay.** The rule uses gross income.
  Applying it to net pay gives a lower figure than the rule intends and makes
  comparisons with landlord requirements confusing.
- **Leaving out debt payments.** Student loans and card minimums don't
  change the 30% or 40× figures, but they reduce what you can actually
  sustain, which is why the calculator recommends the lowest rule.
- **Forgetting costs that come with the lease.** Utilities, parking, renters
  insurance and pet rent add to your monthly housing bill.
- **Ignoring move-in cash.** A security deposit, first month's rent and
  moving costs are due up front, so budget for them separately.
- **Planning around the landlord's minimum.** Qualifying under the 40× rule
  means you pass screening, not that the rent leaves room for savings.

## What Affects How Much Rent You Can Afford

- **Gross income** — every rule scales with it.
- **Existing debt payments** — they reduce the 36% and 50/30/20 figures
  dollar for dollar.
- **Take-home pay** — higher taxes or pre-tax deductions shrink the needs
  budget, though they may be building savings at the same time.
- **Other essentials** — utilities, transport and insurance compete with
  rent for the same part of your budget.
- **Whether utilities are included** — compare rents on the same basis, and
  count separately billed utilities as essentials.
- **Landlord requirements** — if your income falls short of 40×, some
  landlords accept a guarantor, a larger deposit, or proof of savings.
