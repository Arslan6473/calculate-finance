"Should I rent or buy?" is one of the biggest financial decisions most people
make, and most simple comparisons get it wrong in the same way: they treat a
renter's down payment as if it just disappears. This calculator invests that
money instead, which is what actually happens if you don't put it into a
house — and that single choice is often what decides the answer.

## How to Use This Rent vs. Buy Calculator

Enter the home price, down payment, mortgage rate and term, and expected
appreciation under "Buying," along with property tax, insurance,
maintenance, and closing costs. Under "Renting," enter your monthly rent and
expected annual increase. Set how many years you plan to stay — this matters
more than almost any other input — and the return you could earn if your cash
were invested instead of spent on a larger down payment or higher housing
costs.

The results show the net cost of each path over your stay, which one comes
out ahead and by how much, the break-even year if there is one, and a
year-by-year view of home equity against the renter's investment portfolio.

## How the Comparison Is Calculated

Both sides are measured as net cost: everything paid out, minus what you
hold at the end.

```
Net cost of buying  = mortgage payments + tax + insurance + maintenance + HOA
                      + down payment + closing costs
                      − (home value − loan balance − selling costs)

Net cost of renting = rent paid + down payment + closing costs
                      + monthly amounts invested
                      − renter's portfolio value
                    = rent paid − investment growth
```

The calculator runs month by month. Property tax and maintenance are set
each year as a percentage of the home's current value, the home appreciates
gradually through the year, and rent rises once a year. The renter starts
with the buyer's down payment and closing costs invested, that portfolio
compounds monthly at the return you enter, and any month in which owning
costs more than renting, the renter invests the difference, so in those
months both sides pay out the same cash. The renter's net cost is the rent
paid less whatever the invested money earns.

## Why This Comparison Invests the Renter's Cash

A fair comparison has to account for what the buyer's up-front cash would
otherwise do. If someone chooses to rent, their would-be down payment and
closing costs don't vanish — they can be invested. This calculator credits
that growth to the renting side, and does the same each month for the
difference whenever renting costs less than owning that month. Leaving this
out is the most common way rent-vs-buy comparisons overstate how much buying
"builds wealth."

## What "Break-Even Year" Means

It's the first year at which the net cost of buying — after subtracting the
equity you've built and accounting for selling costs — becomes lower than the
net cost of renting — after subtracting the growth on your invested cash.
Staying fewer years than that generally favors renting; staying longer
generally favors buying, because the large up-front and back-end costs of a
home purchase get spread over more years of ownership. If the gap at the end
of your stay is less than 5% of the home price, the result is reported as too
close to call, because small changes in the assumptions could flip it.

## Example Calculation

Take a $400,000 home with $80,000 down, a 6.5% 30-year mortgage, 1.1%
property tax, $1,500 a year in insurance, 1% maintenance, 2% closing costs,
6% selling costs and 3% appreciation. Compare it with $2,200 rent rising 3% a
year, a 10-year stay, and the renter's cash invested at 6%.

- Mortgage payment: **$2,022.62** a month
- First-year cost of owning: $2,022.62 + $366.67 tax + $125 insurance +
  $333.33 maintenance = **$2,847.62** a month, or $647.62 more than rent
- Renter's starting investment: $80,000 + $8,000 closing costs = **$88,000**

After 10 years the home is worth $537,566.55 and the loan balance is
$271,283.60, so selling would leave $234,028.96 after 6% selling costs. The
renter's portfolio has grown to $234,324.39.

- Net cost of buying: **$207,982.03**
- Net cost of renting: **$207,686.59**
- Result: renting is ahead by just **$295.44**, far inside the $20,000
  threshold (5% of the price), so the result is **too close to call**, and
  there is no break-even year inside the 10-year stay

Shorten the stay to 5 years and renting wins clearly, by $21,473.02. Stretch
it to 20 years and buying breaks even in year 11, finishing $92,413.84
ahead. Keep the 10-year stay but raise the rent to $2,800, and buying breaks
even in year 4 and comes out $104,662.18 ahead. Raise the investment return
to 8% instead, and renting comes out $45,628.17 ahead.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Assuming the down payment earns nothing.** If you leave the investment
  return at zero, buying will look far better than it should.
- **Underestimating how long you'll stay.** The stay length moves the answer
  more than any other input, so test a shorter and a longer stay.
- **Ignoring selling costs.** Agent commissions and closing costs on the sale
  come out of your equity, and over a short stay they can outweigh years of
  appreciation.
- **Using optimistic appreciation.** Home prices can stall or fall locally.
  Try a lower rate to see how much your result depends on it.
- **Comparing different homes.** Compare the rent and purchase price of
  similar homes in the same area, or the comparison says little.

## What Affects the Rent vs. Buy Decision

- **How long you'll stay** — the single biggest factor; buying's up-front
  costs need years to amortize away.
- **Home appreciation** — directly increases the equity side of the buying
  calculation.
- **Return on invested cash** — a higher assumed return on the renter's
  invested down payment shifts the comparison toward renting.
- **Rent growth** — faster-rising rent erodes renting's advantage over a
  long enough holding period.

What's not included: one-off costs such as a new roof, mortgage interest
deductions, and the tax on any gain when you sell, which for many homeowners
is reduced by the home sale exclusion.
