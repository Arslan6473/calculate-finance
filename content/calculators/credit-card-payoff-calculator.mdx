Credit card debt behaves differently from a mortgage or auto loan, and that
difference is exactly what makes minimum payments so dangerous. Because the
minimum is usually a percentage of your current balance, it shrinks as your
balance shrinks — so an ever-smaller share of each payment goes to principal,
and payoff can stretch out for decades. This calculator simulates your balance
month by month to show how long a payment plan really takes and what it costs.

## How to Use This Credit Card Payoff Calculator

Enter your current balance and APR, then choose whether you'll pay a fixed
amount each month or the card's minimum payment. For a fixed payment, enter
the amount; for the minimum, enter the percentage and dollar floor your card
uses (check a recent statement — 2% of the balance with a $25 floor is
typical). If you expect to keep charging the card, add an estimated monthly
purchase amount to see its effect on payoff time.

The results show the number of months to reach zero, the total interest, the
total you'll pay, and interest as a percentage of the starting balance. When
you choose a fixed payment, the calculator also runs the minimum-payment plan
on the same balance so you can see exactly what the difference is worth.

## How the Payoff Is Calculated

Credit card interest is charged on the balance each month, so the calculator
works through the debt one month at a time rather than using a single
formula:

```
Each month:
  balance  = balance + new purchases
  interest = balance × (APR ÷ 12)
  balance  = balance + interest
  payment  = your fixed amount, or the minimum:
             the larger of (minimum % × balance) and the dollar floor
  balance  = balance − payment
Repeat until the balance reaches zero, or for at most 600 months.
```

In plain terms: new spending is added first, a month of interest is charged
on the result, and then your payment comes off. The final payment is trimmed
to whatever is left. Card issuers
typically work out interest from daily balances, so your statements may
differ by a few cents, but the timeline and total cost will be very close.

## The Minimum-Payment Trap

A fixed payment stays the same every month, so as your balance falls, a
larger and larger share of it goes to principal — payoff accelerates. A
minimum payment does the opposite: it's recalculated from the current
balance each month, so it shrinks right along with your progress, and an
ever-smaller share goes to principal. On a high-rate balance, this can mean
the payoff timeline stretches past what most people would expect from looking
at the balance alone. Federal law recognizes the problem: since the Credit
CARD Act of 2009, statements must carry a minimum payment warning and show
how long the minimum-only plan would take.

## How This Calculator Determines If Payoff Is Even Possible

Each month, interest is calculated on the current balance and added to it,
then the payment is applied. If the payment doesn't exceed that month's
interest, the balance cannot decrease — it can only grow or stay flat. This
calculator checks for that condition and reports plainly when a payment plan
cannot pay off the balance, rather than showing a misleadingly distant payoff
date. It also stops after 50 years and reports the plan as unable to clear
the balance if it hasn't reached zero by then.

## Example Calculation

Take a $5,000 balance at 22% APR, paid down with a fixed $250 a month and no
new purchases.

- First month's interest: $5,000 × 0.22 ÷ 12 = **$91.67**
- First month's principal: $250 − $91.67 = **$158.33**
- Time to pay off: **26 months**, with a final payment of $35.72
- Total interest: **$1,285.72**, or about 26% of the starting balance
- Total paid: **$6,285.72**

Raising the payment to $350 clears the same balance in 17 months for $851.20
of interest. Dropping it to $150 stretches payoff to 52 months and $2,798.05
of interest. A payment of $90 is below the first month's interest, so the
balance would grow and the calculator reports that it can't be paid off.

Now compare the card's minimum of 2% of the balance with a $25 floor. The
first required payment is just $101.83, and because it falls with the
balance, the debt is still not gone after 50 years: about $1,474 remains
after $35,314.61 of payments, $31,788.93 of which is interest. Continuing to
charge $100 a month while paying a fixed $250, by contrast, still clears the
original balance — but it takes 54 months and $2,961.95 of interest.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Entering a payment below the interest charge.** At 22% APR, a $5,000
  balance accrues about $92 of interest in a month. Any payment at or below
  that amount can never reduce the balance.
- **Guessing the minimum-payment formula.** Issuers calculate minimums in
  different ways, and some add that month's interest and fees to a small
  percentage. Copy the method from your cardholder agreement or statement.
- **Using a promotional rate as the long-term APR.** If a 0% or reduced rate
  is about to expire, run the plan again at the rate that follows it.
- **Forgetting ongoing spending.** A plan that ignores regular purchases on
  the same card will look faster than it really is.
- **Lowering the payment as the balance falls.** Keeping the payment fixed is
  what makes payoff accelerate; letting it drift down with the balance
  recreates the minimum-payment trap.

## What Affects Your Payoff Time and Cost

- **Payment size relative to balance** — the single biggest factor; even a
  modest fixed payment above the minimum can cut years off the payoff time.
- **APR** — credit card rates are often far higher than other consumer debt,
  so interest can dominate the total cost if payoff is slow.
- **New purchases** — ongoing spending on the card competes directly with
  your payment for reducing the balance.
