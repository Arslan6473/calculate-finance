Projecting an investment portfolio means accounting for more than just a
return rate. Fees compound against you the same way returns compound for you,
inflation quietly erodes purchasing power over long horizons, and a rising
contribution can meaningfully change where you land. This calculator models
all three, so the projection reflects what you'd actually experience rather
than an idealized number.

## How to Use This Investment Calculator

Enter your initial investment and planned monthly contribution, an expected
annual return, and how many years you plan to stay invested. In the advanced
section, add an annual fee or expense ratio if your investment carries one, an
annual percentage increase to your contribution (to model rising income), and
an expected inflation rate to see your result in today's purchasing power
alongside the nominal figure. The results show your final balance, total
contributions, total growth, total fees paid, and a year-by-year chart.

## How Investment Growth Is Calculated

Each month, this calculator applies your expected return to the current
balance, deducts any fee from that same balance, and then adds your
contribution. Fees are charged on assets under management — the balance
before that month's contribution — which is how expense ratios actually work
in practice. If you set a contribution increase, the monthly amount rises by
that percentage at the start of each new year.

## Investment Growth Formula

Each month, the balance moves forward by:

```
Balance = Balance × (1 + (R − F) / 12) + C
Real value = Final balance / (1 + I)^Y
```

- **R** is the expected annual return, as a decimal (7% is 0.07).
- **F** is the annual fee or expense ratio, as a decimal. Subtracting it from
  R gives the net return your money actually earns.
- **C** is the monthly contribution for the current year.
- **I** is the annual inflation rate, and **Y** is the number of years.

The second line restates the nominal result in today's money by discounting
it for inflation over the full term.

## The Effect of Fees and Inflation

A 1% annual fee doesn't just cost 1% of your balance in a given year — it
costs 1% of a balance that would otherwise have kept compounding, so the
gap between a low-fee and high-fee portfolio widens every year you stay
invested. Inflation works differently: it doesn't reduce your account
balance, but it reduces what that balance can buy. This calculator reports
both your nominal final balance and that same balance restated in today's
dollars, discounted by your assumed inflation rate.

## Example Calculation

$10,000 invested with a $500 monthly contribution, a 7% expected annual
return, and a 0.5% fee, over 25 years, grows to $424,980.24. You'd have
contributed $160,000, and fees would have cost $20,383.10 along the way.
Assuming 3% average inflation, that balance is worth $202,972.93 in today's
purchasing power — a useful reminder that a large future number and its real
value can differ substantially.

Change the fee and everything else held constant:

- **0.1% fee:** the balance reaches $454,532.18, and fees total $4,268.58.
- **1% fee:** the balance reaches $391,146.68, and fees total $38,524.45.

The gap between a 0.1% and a 1% fee is more than $63,000 on the same
contributions. Separately, raising the contribution by 3% a year (with the
original 0.5% fee) lifts the final balance to $545,904.51, on total
contributions of $228,755.59.

## What Affects Your Investment Outcome

- **Time in the market** — the longest lever available; more years means
  more compounding, and compounding accelerates rather than staying flat.
- **Contribution amount** — for most people investing over decades,
  contributions matter as much as or more than the return rate itself.
- **Contribution growth** — increasing contributions as your income rises
  adds both principal and years of compounding on that principal.
- **Fees** — a seemingly small percentage becomes a large dollar cost over a
  long enough horizon.
- **Inflation** — doesn't change your account balance, but changes what that
  balance is actually worth when you go to spend it. The Federal Reserve aims
  for 2% inflation over the longer run; the 3% default here is a more
  cautious assumption.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Treating the return as guaranteed.** The calculator applies the same
  return every month. Real markets move unevenly, and a bad stretch near the
  end of your horizon affects the result more than one near the start. Run a
  lower-return scenario alongside your main one.
- **Leaving the fee at zero.** Most funds charge an expense ratio, listed in
  the prospectus fee table. Advisory and account fees come on top. Enter the
  total you actually pay.
- **Reading the nominal balance as spending power.** In the example,
  $424,980 in 25 years buys roughly what $203,000 buys today at 3% inflation.
  Plan around the inflation-adjusted figure.
- **Mixing real and nominal assumptions.** If you enter a return that's
  already net of inflation, also entering an inflation rate counts it twice.
  Use a nominal return with an inflation rate, or a real return with
  inflation set to zero.
- **Forgetting taxes.** Gains, dividends, and withdrawals may be taxed
  depending on the account type. This calculator shows pre-tax values.
