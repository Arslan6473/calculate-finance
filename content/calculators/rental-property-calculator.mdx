A rental property can look profitable on rent alone and still lose money
every month once vacancy, repairs, management and the mortgage are counted.
This calculator works through those costs and reports the standard measures
investors use to compare properties: cash flow, net operating income, cap
rate, cash-on-cash return, gross rent multiplier and the 1% rule.

## How to Use This Rental Property Calculator

Enter the purchase price, down payment percentage, closing costs, interest
rate and loan term. Under income, enter the monthly rent you expect when the
unit is let and a vacancy rate for the time it will sit empty or rent goes
unpaid. Under operating expenses, add annual property tax and insurance,
maintenance as a percentage of rent, the property manager's fee (enter 0 if
you manage it yourself), and any HOA or other monthly costs the landlord
pays, such as water or lawn care.

## The Key Numbers and How They Are Calculated

```
Effective rent      = monthly rent × (1 − vacancy %)
Operating expenses  = tax + insurance + maintenance + management + HOA + other
NOI (annual)        = (effective rent − operating expenses) × 12
Cash flow (monthly) = effective rent − operating expenses − mortgage payment
Cap rate            = NOI ÷ purchase price
Cash-on-cash return = annual cash flow ÷ (down payment + closing costs)
Gross rent multiplier = purchase price ÷ annual scheduled rent
1% rule             = monthly rent ≥ 1% of purchase price
```

**Net operating income (NOI)** is income after operating costs but before the
mortgage. It describes the property itself, regardless of how it is
financed.

**Cap rate** divides NOI by price. Because financing is excluded, two buyers
with different loans see the same cap rate on the same property, which makes
it useful for comparing properties with each other.

**Cash flow** subtracts the mortgage payment from NOI. It is what actually
reaches your bank account each month.

**Cash-on-cash return** divides annual cash flow by the cash you put in. It
leaves out principal paydown, appreciation and tax effects — real returns,
but not cash in hand.

**Gross rent multiplier** and the **1% rule** are quick screening tools. They
use rent only and ignore expenses, so they are useful for filtering listings
but not for deciding whether to buy.

Maintenance is calculated on the full scheduled rent, because repairs happen
whether or not the unit is let. Management is calculated on rent actually
collected, which is how most managers bill.

## Example Calculation

Take a $200,000 property bought with 25% down ($50,000) and $6,000 in closing
costs, financed with a $150,000 30-year loan at 7%. It rents for $2,000 a
month with a 5% vacancy allowance. Property tax is $3,000 a year, insurance
$1,200, and both maintenance and management are budgeted at 10%.

| Monthly item | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Scheduled rent | $2,000.00 |
| Vacancy (5%) | −$100.00 |
| **Effective rent** | **$1,900.00** |
| Property tax | −$250.00 |
| Insurance | −$100.00 |
| Maintenance (10% of $2,000) | −$200.00 |
| Management (10% of $1,900) | −$190.00 |
| **Net operating income** | **$1,160.00** |
| Mortgage principal and interest | −$997.95 |
| **Cash flow** | **$162.05** |

Over a year that gives NOI of $13,920 and cash flow of $1,944.60. From there:

- **Cap rate:** $13,920 ÷ $200,000 = **6.96%**
- **Cash-on-cash return:** $1,944.60 ÷ $56,000 = **3.47%**
- **Gross rent multiplier:** $200,000 ÷ $24,000 = **8.33**
- **1% rule:** $2,000 is exactly 1% of $200,000, so it **passes**
- **Debt service coverage:** $13,920 ÷ $11,975.40 of annual mortgage payments
  = **1.16**

Compare a $300,000 property renting for $2,500 with 20% down and $9,000 in
closing costs, also at 7% over 30 years, with $3,600 in tax, $1,500 in
insurance and 8% each for maintenance and management. Its cap rate is a
respectable 6.24%, but the larger loan costs $1,596.73 a month and cash flow
comes out at **−$36.73** a month. Rent at 0.83% of price also misses the 1%
rule. A higher price with proportionally lower rent leaves less room to
cover the mortgage.

## Taxes Are Not Included

Every figure here is before income tax. Rental income is taxable, but
ordinary operating expenses and mortgage interest are generally deductible
against it, and the building itself — not the land — is depreciated over
time. Under the general system, residential rental buildings are depreciated
over 27.5 years. The result can be a taxable figure quite different from
your cash flow, so check the IRS rules or a tax professional before relying
on after-tax returns.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Leaving vacancy at zero.** Even a well-located unit sits empty between
  tenants, and turnover usually brings cleaning and repair costs too.
- **Treating principal as an expense.** Only the interest part of the
  mortgage is a cost; the principal builds equity. Cash flow still subtracts
  the full payment because it is cash leaving your account.
- **Using the seller's expense figures.** Get your own insurance quote and
  check the property tax that will apply after the sale, which can be
  higher than what the current owner pays.
- **Skipping management because you'll self-manage.** Budgeting a fee keeps
  the analysis honest about the value of your time, and covers you if you
  hire a manager later.
- **Relying on the 1% rule alone.** It ignores every expense, so a property
  can pass it and still lose money after tax, insurance and repairs.

## What Affects Rental Property Returns

- **Purchase price relative to rent** — the biggest driver of every metric;
  paying more for the same rent lowers cap rate, cash flow and returns.
- **Interest rate and down payment** — they change cash flow and
  cash-on-cash return, but not NOI or cap rate.
- **Vacancy** — each month empty is roughly 8% of a year's rent gone.
- **Maintenance and capital expenses** — older properties often need more
  than 10%; roofs, heating systems and appliances all need replacing
  eventually.
- **Management** — self-managing saves the fee but costs your time.
- **Property tax and insurance** — these vary widely by location and can
  rise after purchase as the property is reassessed.
