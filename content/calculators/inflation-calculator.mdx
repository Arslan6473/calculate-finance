Inflation is the gradual rise in the general level of prices, and its
effect is easy to underestimate because it happens a little at a time. A
few percent a year barely registers at the grocery store, but compounded
over a decade or two it changes what your savings, salary, and retirement
income can actually buy. This calculator shows that effect in dollars: what
today's purchases will cost in the future, how much buying power your money
will lose, and what today's amount was equivalent to in the past.

## How to Use This Inflation Calculator

First choose what you want to know. **Future** mode projects forward: enter
an amount in today's dollars, an annual inflation rate, and a number of
years to see what the same goods will cost then and what your money will be
worth in today's terms. **Past** mode looks back: it shows what today's
amount was equivalent to a given number of years ago at the rate you enter.

The default inflation rate is 3%. You can change it to any rate you want to
test, including a negative rate for deflation. Results include cumulative
inflation over the whole period, the share of purchasing power lost, and a
chart of how prices and buying power move year by year.

## How Inflation Is Calculated

Inflation compounds just like interest. Each year's price increase applies
to prices that already include every earlier year's increases, so a
constant rate produces accelerating growth in dollar terms. The calculator
applies a single assumed rate every year. Projecting forward multiplies by
the compounding factor; looking back, or measuring purchasing power,
divides by it.

## Inflation Formula

```
Future cost       = Amount × (1 + r)^n
Purchasing power  = Amount ÷ (1 + r)^n
Cumulative inflation  = (1 + r)^n − 1
Purchasing power lost = 1 − 1 ÷ (1 + r)^n
```

**r** is the annual inflation rate as a decimal and **n** is the number of
years. Past mode uses the purchasing power line: an amount today divided by
(1 + r)^n is what it was equivalent to n years ago.

## Example Calculation

Take $1,000 at 3% annual inflation over 10 years. The compounding factor is
1.03^10 ≈ 1.3439.

- **Future cost**: $1,000 × 1.3439 = **$1,343.92**. Goods that cost $1,000
  today would cost about $1,344 in ten years.
- **Purchasing power**: $1,000 ÷ 1.3439 = **$744.09**. Ten years from now,
  $1,000 will buy only what $744.09 buys today.
- **Cumulative inflation**: prices rise **34.39%** in total — more than the
  30% you would get by simply adding up ten years of 3%.
- **Purchasing power lost**: **25.59%**. The loss is smaller than the price
  rise because each dollar now buys 1 ÷ 1.3439 of what it did.

Looking back instead, $100 today at the same 3% rate was equivalent to about
**$55.37** twenty years ago.

## Why This Calculator Uses an Assumed Rate

Official inflation figures in the United States come from the Consumer
Price Index, published monthly by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. The
CPI is the right source for exact historical comparisons, such as what a
specific amount from a specific year is worth today. This calculator
deliberately does not embed a copy of those tables, because a stored copy
goes out of date every month and cannot be checked at a glance. Instead it
uses a rate you choose, which makes the assumption visible and is the
natural tool for planning ahead, where no official figure exists yet. For a
precise historical answer, look up the CPI values for the two dates
directly from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

## Factors to Consider When Choosing a Rate

- **The Federal Reserve's target** — the Fed aims for 2% inflation over the
  longer run, measured by the price index for personal consumption
  expenditures. It is a target, not a guarantee.
- **Recent experience** — inflation has run well above and below 2% in
  different periods. Testing a higher rate shows how sensitive a long-term
  plan is.
- **Your own spending** — the official indexes measure a broad basket of
  goods and services. Costs that matter most to you, such as housing,
  health care, or tuition, can rise faster or slower than the average.
- **Time horizon** — small differences in the rate compound into large
  differences over 20 or 30 years.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Adding instead of compounding** — 3% for 10 years is 34.39% in total,
  not 30%. The gap grows with longer periods and higher rates.
- **Confusing price rise with purchasing power loss** — a 34.39% price
  increase is a 25.59% loss of buying power, not a 34.39% loss.
- **Planning in today's dollars only** — a retirement or college target
  that ignores inflation will fall short of the real cost.
- **Assuming one average rate fits every expense** — some categories inflate
  much faster than the overall index.
- **Using an assumed rate for exact history** — for a precise past
  comparison, use official CPI figures rather than an average rate.
