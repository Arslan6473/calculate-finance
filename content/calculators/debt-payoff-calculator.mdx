When you're juggling more than one debt, the order you pay them off in
changes both how fast you're debt-free and how much interest you pay along
the way. This calculator simulates two common strategies — snowball and
avalanche — so you can see the real difference in numbers rather than
guessing.

## How to Use This Debt Payoff Calculator

Add each debt you're paying down, with its balance, interest rate, and
minimum payment. Choose a strategy — avalanche targets the highest interest
rate first, snowball targets the smallest balance first — and enter how much
extra you can put toward debt each month beyond the minimums. The calculator
simulates every debt month by month under your chosen strategy and shows the
comparison against the alternative: your debt-free date, total interest,
total paid, and the month each debt is cleared.

## How the Snowball Effect Works

Every dollar of minimum payment keeps flowing even after a debt is paid off —
it doesn't disappear, it rolls into the extra payment attacking whichever debt
is next in line. That rollover is what makes both strategies accelerate over
time rather than progressing at a constant pace, and it's the main reason
either one beats paying only minimums on every debt indefinitely.

## How the Payoff Is Calculated

Each month, the simulation runs these steps:

```
Interest on each debt = Balance × (APR ÷ 12)
Monthly budget        = Sum of all original minimums + Extra payment
Target payment        = Budget − Minimums paid on every other debt
```

- **Balance** is what's left on a debt after last month's payment, and
  **APR** is its annual interest rate as a decimal.
- **Sum of all original minimums** stays in the budget even after a debt is
  cleared — that's the rollover.
- **Target** is the first debt in the strategy's order: highest APR for
  avalanche, smallest balance for snowball. When two debts tie, avalanche
  picks the smaller balance and snowball the higher rate.

Interest is added to every debt first, then each non-target debt gets its
minimum, and everything left goes to the target. The simulation repeats until
every balance reaches zero. Before it starts, the calculator checks whether
your total monthly payment exceeds the interest accruing across all debts.
If it doesn't, the balance can never shrink, and the calculator says so
rather than showing a payoff date that doesn't exist.

## Snowball vs. Avalanche

Avalanche orders debts by interest rate, highest first, which is
mathematically optimal — it minimizes the total interest paid across all your
debts. Snowball orders debts by balance, smallest first, which usually costs
a bit more in interest but produces a fully paid-off debt sooner. Many people
find that early win easier to stay motivated by, even though it's not the
lowest-cost path in pure dollar terms.

## Example Calculation

Take three debts with $260 in combined minimums and $200 extra a month, for
$460 in total:

- A $6,000 credit card at 24% APR, $150 minimum.
- A $2,500 personal loan at 11% APR, $80 minimum.
- A $900 store card at 19% APR, $30 minimum.

**Avalanche** attacks the 24% credit card first and clears it in month 22,
then the store card in month 24 and the personal loan in month 27. Total
interest: $2,089.93.

**Snowball** clears the $900 store card in month 5 and the personal loan in
month 13, then finishes the credit card in month 27. Total interest:
$2,609.86.

Both are debt-free in 27 months, but snowball costs $519.93 more, because the
24% card gets only its minimum payment for the first 13 months. The extra $200
is what drives the timeline: with no extra payment, avalanche takes 59 months
and $5,787.96 in interest on the same debts.

Order doesn't always matter. When the smallest balance also carries the
highest rate, the next-smallest the next-highest, and so on, both strategies
pick the same targets and produce identical results. The calculator's
starting debts are an example: either way, they're cleared in 41 months with
$3,496.63 in total interest.

## What Determines How Much a Strategy Saves

- **Spread between interest rates** — when your highest-rate debt is far
  above the others, avalanche's savings over snowball are largest.
- **Where the high rate sits** — the gap is widest when the highest-rate
  debt is also the largest, as in the example, because snowball leaves it
  for last.
- **Extra payment size** — a larger extra payment shortens payoff time for
  both strategies and shrinks the dollar gap between them.
- **Number of debts** — more debts mean more rollover events, amplifying the
  snowball effect for whichever strategy you choose.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Spending a freed-up minimum payment.** When a debt is cleared, its
  minimum must roll into the next target. Absorbing it into everyday
  spending removes the effect that makes both strategies work.
- **Entering the current minimum as if it were fixed.** Credit card minimums
  usually fall as the balance falls. The simulation keeps your total payment
  at the starting level, so to match it, keep paying at least that amount.
- **Adding new charges while paying down.** The simulation assumes balances
  only go down. New purchases on a card you're paying off push the debt-free
  date back.
- **Choosing snowball without checking the cost.** Snowball's quick wins
  have a price that depends on your debts — $519.93 in the example. Compare
  both results before committing, so you know what the motivation costs.
- **Ignoring promotional rates that expire.** A 0% balance-transfer or
  introductory rate will rise when the promotion ends. Enter the rate you'll
  actually pay, or rerun the plan when it changes.
