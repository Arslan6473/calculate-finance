A raise is easy to describe and surprisingly easy to misjudge. A 4% increase
sounds good until you notice prices rose 3% over the same year. This
calculator shows your new salary, what the raise means for each paycheck,
and — if you enter an inflation rate — how much of it is a real gain in
purchasing power.

## How to Use This Pay Raise Calculator

Enter your current annual salary, then choose how your raise was given: as a
percentage or as a dollar amount per year. Fill in the matching field — the
other one is ignored. Pick how often you're paid so the calculator can show
the increase per paycheck. Finally, enter an expected inflation rate if you
want to see whether the raise keeps up with rising prices; set it to zero to
skip that part.

## How the Raise Is Calculated

The basic figures follow directly from your salary and the raise:

```
Raise amount          = current salary × raise %   (or the dollar amount)
New salary            = current salary + raise amount
Raise percentage      = raise amount ÷ current salary × 100
Monthly increase      = raise amount ÷ 12
Per-paycheck increase = raise amount ÷ paychecks per year
```

Paychecks per year are 52 for weekly pay, 26 for biweekly, 24 for
twice-monthly, and 12 for monthly. Whichever way you enter the raise, the
calculator converts it to the other form, so you can compare a percentage
offer with a flat-dollar one on equal terms.

## The Real, Inflation-Adjusted Raise

A raise only improves your standard of living to the extent it outpaces
inflation. The exact relationship is:

```
Real raise = (1 + nominal raise) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1
```

A common shortcut is to subtract inflation from the raise, and for small
numbers it's close. But it always overstates the real gain a little, and the
error grows as rates rise. This calculator uses the exact formula. If the
real raise is negative, your pay has gone up on paper but buys less than it
did before.

## Example Calculation

Say you earn $60,000 a year, are paid every two weeks, and receive a 4%
raise while inflation runs at 3%.

- Raise amount: $60,000 × 0.04 = **$2,400**
- New salary: $60,000 + $2,400 = **$62,400**
- Monthly increase: $2,400 ÷ 12 = **$200**
- Per-paycheck increase: $2,400 ÷ 26 = **$92.31**
- Real raise: 1.04 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = **0.97%**

The shortcut would have said 1%, which is slightly too generous. In today's
money, your new $62,400 salary is worth about $62,400 ÷ 1.03 = $60,582.52 —
a real improvement of a little under $600.

If the same employer had offered a flat $2,500 instead, that would be
$2,500 ÷ $60,000 = 4.17%, slightly better than the 4% offer. It would add
$96.15 to each biweekly paycheck and give a real raise of 1.13% after 3%
inflation.

## Gross Increase vs. Take-Home Increase

Every figure here is before tax. Part of your raise goes to federal income
tax, Social Security, Medicare, and any state tax, and because a raise is
added on top of your existing income, it's taxed at your marginal rate — the
rate of your highest bracket. Your take-home pay will rise by less than the
gross figures shown. To compare the after-tax difference, run your old and
new salary through the salary calculator.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Expecting the whole raise in your paycheck.** The raise is taxed at your
  marginal rate, and pre-tax deductions such as a percentage-based
  retirement contribution also rise with your pay.
- **Using last year's inflation without checking it.** Look up the change in
  the Consumer Price Index over the period your raise covers rather than
  guessing; a one-point difference in inflation changes the real result by
  about the same amount.
- **Comparing a percentage with a dollar figure by eye.** A $2,500 raise on
  $60,000 is 4.17%; on $80,000 it is only 3.13%. Convert both offers to the
  same terms first.
- **Ignoring when the raise starts.** A raise that takes effect partway
  through the year adds only part of the annual amount to that year's pay.
- **Forgetting that a promotion may change other pay.** Moving to a salaried
  or exempt role can end paid overtime, which may cancel out part of the
  increase.

## What to Consider When Evaluating a Raise

- **Inflation** — a raise below the inflation rate is a cut in purchasing
  power, even though the number on your paycheck goes up.
- **Compounding** — each raise is applied to a salary that already includes
  past raises, so small differences add up over a career.
- **Total compensation** — changes to bonus, retirement matching, or health
  insurance contributions can matter as much as the base salary.
- **Market rates** — what's typical varies by field, employer, and year, so
  compare against data for your own role rather than a single national
  average.
