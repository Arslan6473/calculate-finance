Overtime can add a meaningful amount to an hourly worker's income, but it's
easy to lose track of what a few extra hours each week are really worth over
a year. This calculator works out your overtime rate, your weekly pay with
overtime, and what that schedule adds up to annually.

## How to Use This Overtime Pay Calculator

Enter your regular hourly rate and the hours you work at that rate each
week — 40 for most full-time jobs. Then enter your overtime hours per week
and choose your overtime rate: time and a half (1.5×) or double time (2×).
Finally, enter how many weeks a year you expect to work this schedule. If
overtime only happens during a busy season, enter just those weeks, or use an
average weekly overtime figure across the whole year.

## How Overtime Pay Is Calculated

The calculation has four steps:

```
Overtime rate    = regular hourly rate × overtime multiplier
Regular pay      = regular hourly rate × regular hours
Overtime pay     = overtime rate × overtime hours
Total weekly pay = regular pay + overtime pay
```

Annual figures multiply the weekly amounts by the number of weeks you entered.
The calculator also shows your effective hourly rate — total weekly pay
divided by total hours worked — which shows how much overtime lifts your
average earnings per hour.

## US Overtime Rules in Brief

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), employers must pay
non-exempt employees at least one and a half times their regular rate of pay
for every hour worked over 40 in a workweek. A few points are worth knowing:

- **The workweek is the unit.** Overtime is based on hours within a single,
  fixed seven-day workweek. Federal law doesn't let an employer average a
  long week against a short one.
- **Exempt employees aren't covered.** Certain salaried employees in
  executive, administrative, and professional roles who meet both a salary
  threshold and a duties test are exempt. Job title alone doesn't decide it.
- **Double time isn't federally required.** It comes from some state laws,
  union contracts, or employer policies.
- **States can go further.** Where state law is more generous, the more
  generous rule applies. California, for example, also requires overtime for
  hours beyond eight in a single workday, not just beyond 40 in a week.

This calculator takes your overtime hours as you enter them, so it works
under any of these rules — enter the hours your employer actually pays at the
overtime rate. This is general information, not legal advice; your state
labor department or the US Department of Labor can confirm the rules that
apply to your job.

## Example Calculation

Suppose you earn $22 an hour, work a regular 40-hour week, and put in 8 hours
of overtime at time and a half, every week of the year.

- Overtime rate: $22 × 1.5 = **$33 an hour**
- Regular pay: $22 × 40 = **$880**
- Overtime pay: $33 × 8 = **$264**
- Total weekly pay: $880 + $264 = **$1,144**
- Annual total: $1,144 × 52 = **$59,488**, of which $13,728 is overtime
- Effective hourly rate: $1,144 ÷ 48 hours = **$23.83**

If the same 8 hours were paid at double time, the overtime rate would be $44,
overtime pay $352 a week, and total weekly pay $1,232.

## Gross Pay and Tax

All figures are gross pay, before tax. Overtime is paid as wages, so Social
Security, Medicare, and income tax withholding apply to it. Large overtime
paychecks can look heavily withheld, but your actual income tax is settled on
your annual income when you file. Federal law also provides a temporary
deduction for the premium portion of FLSA-required overtime for tax years
2025 through 2028, subject to limits and income phase-outs — check current
IRS guidance for details. The salary calculator can estimate take-home pay
from your annual total.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Counting the same hours twice.** If you work 48 hours, enter 40 regular
  hours and 8 overtime hours, not 48 and 8.
- **Using base pay when the regular rate is higher.** Under the FLSA, the
  regular rate includes all pay for employment apart from specific
  exclusions such as gifts and discretionary bonuses, so a production bonus
  can push your overtime rate above 1.5 times your base wage.
- **Annualizing a busy season.** Multiplying a peak week by 52 overstates
  annual pay if overtime only happens part of the year.
- **Averaging hours across two weeks.** Forty hours one week and 50 the next
  is 10 overtime hours under the federal rule, not zero.
- **Assuming the tax deduction covers all overtime pay.** The federal
  deduction applies only to the premium portion of FLSA-required overtime,
  such as the half in time and a half, up to annual limits.

## What Affects Your Overtime Pay

- **Your regular rate** — the overtime rate is a multiple of it, so every
  dollar of base pay is worth 1.5 or 2 dollars per overtime hour.
- **Your multiplier** — double time pays a third more per overtime hour than
  time and a half.
- **How consistent overtime is** — annual figures assume the same schedule
  every week you enter, so seasonal overtime should use fewer weeks.
- **Your exemption status** — exempt salaried employees generally aren't owed
  overtime at all under federal law.
