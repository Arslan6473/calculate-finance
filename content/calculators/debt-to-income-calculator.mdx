Your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, is the share of your gross monthly income
that goes to required debt payments. It is one of the first numbers a
mortgage lender checks, and many auto and personal lenders look at it too.
This calculator works out both versions lenders use, compares the result
with common guidelines, and tells you how much monthly debt you could add,
or would need to cut, to reach a 36% ratio.

## How to Use This DTI Calculator

Enter your gross monthly income, meaning pay before taxes and deductions,
plus any other regular income you can document, such as side work,
alimony received or rental income. Then enter your monthly housing payment:
rent, or your full mortgage payment including property tax, insurance and
HOA dues. Finally, add the required monthly payments on car loans, student
loans, credit cards and any other debts.

Use minimum required payments, not balances and not what you choose to
pay. Everyday expenses like groceries, utilities, phone bills and car
insurance are not debts and do not belong in DTI.

## How DTI Is Calculated

```
Total income      = Gross monthly income + Other monthly income
Front-end DTI     = Housing payment ÷ Total income × 100
Back-end DTI      = All monthly debt payments ÷ Total income × 100
Debt at 36%       = Total income × 0.36
Room or shortfall = Debt at 36% − All monthly debt payments
```

The **front-end** ratio counts housing alone. The **back-end** ratio counts
housing plus every other required debt payment, and it is the number most
people mean when they say DTI. A positive room figure is how much monthly
debt you could add before reaching 36%; a negative one is how much you
would need to cut.

## Common DTI Guidelines

There is no single cutoff, but two reference points come up constantly:

- **36%** — a long-standing conventional guideline for the back-end ratio.
  Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, for example, sets 36% as the maximum for
  manually underwritten loans, rising to 45% with strong credit and
  reserves, and up to 50% for loans run through its automated system.
- **43%** — the back-end limit originally written into the Qualified
  Mortgage rules. The current general Qualified Mortgage definition uses a
  pricing test instead, but 43% is still widely quoted as a reference.

The calculator places your back-end ratio in one of three bands: at or
below 36%, from 36% to 43%, or above 43%. These are common guidelines, not
approval decisions. Lenders vary, loan programs differ, and your credit
score, savings and down payment all matter too.

## Example Calculation

Suppose you earn $7,000 a month before tax and bring in another $500 a
month from regular freelance work, for a total of **$7,500**. Your monthly
payments are $1,900 for rent, $450 for a car loan, $300 for student loans
and $150 in credit card minimums, for a total of **$2,800**.

- Front-end DTI: $1,900 ÷ $7,500 = **25.33%**
- Back-end DTI: $2,800 ÷ $7,500 = **37.33%**
- Debt at 36%: $7,500 × 0.36 = $2,700
- Shortfall: $2,700 − $2,800 = **$100 a month to cut**

A 37.33% ratio sits in the 36% to 43% band: above the conventional
guideline but below the 43% reference. Now suppose you pay off the car
loan. Total debt drops to $2,350, the back-end ratio falls to **31.33%**,
and you would have **$350 a month** of room before reaching 36%.

## What Affects Your DTI

- **Income you can document** — lenders count gross, verifiable income;
  irregular cash that does not show up on tax returns usually does not
  count.
- **Housing cost** — usually the largest single item, and the whole of the
  front-end ratio.
- **Installment loans** — car and student loan payments stay in the ratio
  until the loan is paid off, so retiring a small loan removes its full
  payment at once.
- **Credit card minimums** — they rise and fall with your balances, so
  paying cards down lowers DTI.
- **New debt** — a new car loan or financed purchase shortly before a
  mortgage application can push you over a lender's limit.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Using take-home pay.** DTI is based on gross income; using net pay
  makes your ratio look worse than a lender would calculate.
- **Entering balances instead of payments.** A $6,000 card balance with a
  $150 minimum counts as $150.
- **Leaving out debts.** Child support, alimony paid and personal loans
  count, even though they may not appear on your credit report.
- **Treating 36% or 43% as hard limits.** They are common guidelines;
  some lenders approve more, others less.
- **Confusing approval with affordability.** A ratio a lender accepts can
  still feel tight once taxes, savings and living costs come out of your
  pay.
