"How much house can I afford?" usually means "how much will a lender let me
borrow?" — and lenders answer that mostly with two debt-to-income ratios. This
calculator applies both, works out which one actually limits you, and solves
for the highest home price whose full monthly payment fits: principal,
interest, property tax, insurance and HOA, not just the loan payment.

## How to Use This Home Affordability Calculator

Enter your gross annual income — before tax, the way a lender counts it — and
the minimum monthly payments on any existing debts such as a car loan,
student loans or credit cards. Leave out rent, since the new housing payment
replaces it. Add the cash you plan to put down, the interest rate and loan
term you expect, and your local property tax rate, home insurance cost and
any HOA fee. The two DTI limits default to 28% and 36%; change them if your
lender or loan program uses different numbers.

## The 28/36 Rule

The 28/36 rule is a long-standing guideline for conventional mortgages:

- **Front-end ratio (28%)** — your total housing payment should be no more
  than 28% of gross monthly income.
- **Back-end ratio (36%)** — your housing payment *plus* all other monthly
  debt payments should be no more than 36% of gross monthly income.

Both have to be satisfied, so the one that leaves less room for housing is
the one that binds. With little existing debt, the 28% rule usually binds.
Once other debt payments exceed 8% of income, the 36% rule takes over.

These are guidelines, not law. Fannie Mae, for example, caps total DTI at
36% for manually underwritten loans but allows up to 45% with qualifying
credit and reserves, and up to 50% through its automated underwriting.
Approval also depends on credit score, cash reserves and your down payment.
Treat the result as a realistic starting point, then check with a lender.

## How the Maximum Price Is Calculated

The monthly cost of a home rises in a straight line with its price, which
means the price can be solved for exactly:

```
Max price = D + (B − I − H − t × D) ÷ (f + t)
```

Where **B** is the monthly housing budget from the binding rule, **D** is the
down payment, **I** and **H** are monthly insurance and HOA, **t** is the
monthly property tax rate (annual rate ÷ 12), and **f** is the monthly loan
payment per dollar borrowed from the standard amortization formula. The
result is rounded down to the dollar so the payment never exceeds the limit.

## Example Calculation

Take a household earning $100,000 a year with $500 a month in car and
student loan payments, $60,000 saved for a down payment, a 6.5% 30-year
mortgage, a 1.1% property tax rate, $1,500 a year in insurance and no HOA.

Gross monthly income is $8,333.33. The 28% front-end limit allows $2,333.33
for housing. The 36% back-end limit allows $3,000 for all debts, which
leaves $2,500 for housing after the $500 of existing payments. The front-end
limit is lower, so it binds.

Solving for the price gives a maximum of **$357,530**, with a loan of
$297,530. The monthly payment at that price breaks down as:

| Component | Monthly |
| --- | --- |
| Principal and interest | $1,880.59 |
| Property tax | $327.74 |
| Home insurance | $125.00 |
| **Total** | **$2,333.33** |

Now raise the existing debt payments to $1,000 a month. The back-end limit
drops to $3,000 − $1,000 = $2,000, below the $2,333.33 front-end limit, so
the 36% rule binds and the maximum price falls to **$311,473**.

The $60,000 down payment in the first case is about 16.8% of the price,
below 20%, so most conventional lenders would also charge private mortgage
insurance. That cost is not included here and would lower the maximum
slightly.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Entering take-home pay instead of gross income.** Both ratios are
  measured against income before tax, so using net pay understates what a
  lender would allow, and the comparison no longer matches the rule.
- **Leaving out small debts.** A $150 minimum card payment counts toward the
  back-end limit just like a car loan, and once that limit binds it cuts the
  housing budget by the same $150.
- **Treating the maximum as the target.** The result is the most the
  ratios allow, not what fits your budget once childcare, savings and
  repairs are counted.
- **Forgetting mortgage insurance.** With less than 20% down, a conventional
  loan usually adds a monthly premium that this calculation doesn't include.
- **Spending the whole down payment.** Closing costs, moving and early
  repairs also need cash, so keep some savings back instead of putting every
  dollar into the price.

## What Affects How Much House You Can Afford

- **Income** — both limits are percentages of gross income, so the budget
  scales directly with what you earn.
- **Existing debt payments** — every dollar of monthly debt above the gap
  between the two limits removes a dollar from your housing budget.
- **Interest rate** — at 6.5% a 30-year loan costs about $6.32 a month per
  $1,000 borrowed; at 7.5% it is about $6.99, which shrinks the loan you can
  carry by roughly a tenth.
- **Down payment** — cash adds to the price one-for-one, and a larger share
  down can also avoid PMI.
- **Property tax, insurance and HOA** — these use part of the same monthly
  budget, so high-tax areas or condo fees reduce the price you can afford.
- **Loan term** — a 15-year loan has a lower rate but a much higher payment,
  so it supports a lower price than a 30-year loan on the same income.
