Whether it's a personal loan, an auto loan, or a business term loan, most
fixed-rate instalment loans work the same way under the hood: a level monthly
payment that covers interest on the current balance and steadily pays down
principal until the loan reaches zero. This calculator works for any of them —
and it also surfaces something the interest rate alone won't tell you: the
effective APR once an origination fee is factored in.

## How to Use This Loan Calculator

Enter the amount you're borrowing, the annual interest rate (APR), and the
term — from 2 to 7 years. If your loan includes an origination fee — common
with personal loans — enter it in dollars; the calculator shows how much cash
you'll actually receive and what that fee does to your true cost of
borrowing. Add an extra monthly payment to see how much sooner you'd be
debt-free and how much interest you'd avoid. The results include your
monthly payment, total interest, total repaid, interest as a percentage of
the amount borrowed, and a full amortization schedule.

## How Loan Payments Are Calculated

Like a mortgage, an instalment loan is amortized: the payment stays level, but
the interest and principal portions shift every month as the balance falls.
The amount of interest owed each month is simply the remaining balance
multiplied by the monthly rate, so a larger balance early in the loan means a
larger interest share of that month's payment. Whatever is left of the
payment after interest reduces the balance.

## Loan Payment Formula

```
M = L × [ i(1 + i)^n ] / [ (1 + i)^n − 1 ]
```

- **M** is the scheduled monthly payment.
- **L** is the loan amount — the full amount you owe, including any
  origination fee deducted from your proceeds.
- **i** is the monthly interest rate: the annual rate ÷ 12, as a decimal.
- **n** is the number of monthly payments in the term.

When an origination fee is present, the effective APR is solved separately.
It's the annual rate at which the present value of your payment stream equals
the cash you actually received, rather than the amount you're contractually
repaying. There's no closed-form solution, so the calculator finds that rate
numerically. An extra monthly payment doesn't change the effective APR: it
shortens the loan, but the cost of the credit you were offered is measured
against the scheduled payment.

## Example Calculation

A $25,000 loan at 8% APR over 5 years (60 months) has a monthly payment of
$506.91, with total interest of $5,414.59 — about 21.7% of the amount
borrowed.

Add a $1,000 origination fee, and the amount actually disbursed drops to
$24,000, while the required payment stays at $506.91, because it's still
based on the full $25,000 owed. Measured against the cash you received, the
effective APR is 9.74%. A smaller $500 fee produces an effective APR of
8.86%.

Two other variations on the same loan:

- **Pay an extra $100 a month.** The loan is repaid in 49 months instead of
  60, and total interest falls to $4,325.29 — about $1,089 less.
- **Stretch the term to 7 years (84 months).** The payment drops to $389.66,
  but total interest rises to $7,731.05, roughly 43% more than the 5-year
  loan.

## What Affects the Total Cost of a Loan

- **Interest rate** — the single biggest lever on total interest paid. Rates
  also differ by loan type: the Federal Reserve's consumer credit release
  reports average personal loan rates well above new-car loan rates.
- **Term length** — a longer term lowers the monthly payment but increases
  total interest, since the balance stays higher for longer.
- **Origination fees** — reduce the cash you receive without reducing what
  you owe, which is why comparing loans by APR (not just the interest rate)
  matters.
- **Extra payments** — any amount paid above the required payment goes
  straight to principal, shortening the loan and cutting future interest.
- **Loan amount** — the payment and total interest both scale in proportion
  to what you borrow, so borrowing only what you need is the simplest saving.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Comparing offers by interest rate instead of APR.** Two loans at 8% can
  cost very different amounts if one deducts a fee upfront. Under the Truth
  in Lending rules, prepaid finance charges such as origination fees are
  reflected in the disclosed APR, so APR is the fairer comparison.
- **Borrowing exactly what you need when a fee is deducted.** If you need
  $25,000 in hand and the lender deducts a $1,000 fee, a $25,000 loan leaves
  you $1,000 short. Check the disbursed amount, not just the loan amount.
- **Choosing the longest term to get the lowest payment.** In the example,
  going from 5 to 7 years cuts the payment by about $117 a month but adds
  more than $2,300 in interest.
- **Assuming extra payments are applied to principal.** Confirm with your
  lender how extra money is credited, and whether the loan carries a
  prepayment penalty, before relying on the savings shown here.
- **Ignoring how interest is front-loaded.** Because interest is charged on
  the current balance, early payments are interest-heavy. Paying a loan off
  halfway through the term saves less interest than half the total.
