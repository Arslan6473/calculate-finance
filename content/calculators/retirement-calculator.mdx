"Am I saving enough for retirement?" is really two questions: how much will
you have, and how much will you need. This calculator answers both — and then
goes a step further by simulating what happens after retirement, when your
portfolio switches from growing to being drawn down.

## How to Use This Retirement Calculator

Enter your current age, planned retirement age, current savings, and monthly
contribution, along with any employer match as a percentage of your own
contribution (a 50% match on an $800 contribution adds $400 a month). In the
assumptions section, set your expected returns before and after retirement
(often lower after, reflecting a more conservative portfolio), inflation, your
desired annual income in retirement in today's dollars, and the age you want
your plan to cover.

## How This Calculator Determines If You're "On Track"

First, it projects your balance forward to your retirement date using your
contributions, employer match, and expected pre-retirement return. Separately,
it takes your desired income — entered in today's dollars — and inflates it
to what that same purchasing power will cost by your retirement date. From
there, it calculates the lump sum needed at retirement to fund that inflating
income stream for every year until your planned life expectancy, given your
expected post-retirement return. If your projected balance meets or exceeds
that target, you're on track.

## Retirement Formulas

```
Balance each month = Balance × (1 + Rpre / 12) + C × (1 + M)
Income at retirement = D × (1 + g)^Y
Target = Income × [ 1 − ((1 + g) / (1 + Rpost))^N ] / (Rpost − g) × (1 + Rpost)
```

- **Rpre** and **Rpost** are your expected annual returns before and after
  retirement, as decimals.
- **C** is your monthly contribution and **M** is the employer match as a
  decimal (50% is 0.5).
- **D** is your desired annual income in today's dollars, **g** is the
  inflation rate, and **Y** is the number of years until retirement.
- **N** is the number of years from retirement to your life expectancy.

The third line is the present value of a growing annuity due: the lump sum
that can pay an income rising with inflation each year for N years, with each
withdrawal taken at the start of the year, while the unspent balance keeps
earning the post-retirement return. When the
post-retirement return equals inflation, the target is simply income × N.

## Why the Drawdown Phase Matters

A portfolio in retirement is simultaneously growing (from continued
investment return) and shrinking (from withdrawals that themselves rise with
inflation each year). Whether the money lasts depends on the balance between
those two forces, which is why this calculator simulates retirement year by
year rather than applying a flat withdrawal-rate rule. Each year's withdrawal
is taken at the start of the year and the remainder grows at your
post-retirement return. If withdrawals outpace growth, it reports the age
your money is projected to run out.

## Example Calculation

Take someone aged 30 with $40,000 saved, contributing $800 a month with a
50% employer match, expecting 7% before retirement and 5% after, 3%
inflation, and a $60,000 income in today's dollars through age 90.

By 65 the projected balance is $2,621,511.60. Of that, $376,000 is their own
money (including the $40,000 starting balance), $168,000 is employer match,
and the rest is investment growth. But 35 years of 3% inflation turns the
$60,000 income goal into $168,831.75 a year at retirement, and funding that
rising income for 25 years requires $3,383,275.60. The plan is $761,764.00
short, and the year-by-year simulation projects the money running out at
age 84.

Small changes move the result a lot. Retiring at 67 instead lifts the
balance to $3,045,047.04 and shrinks the shortfall to $316,285.70, with the
money lasting to 88. Without the employer match, the balance at 65 is only
$1,901,089.75, and the money runs out at 78.

## What Affects Your Retirement Outlook

- **Time until retirement** — more years means more compounding on both your
  contributions and your employer's match.
- **Return assumptions** — small changes to expected return compound
  significantly over a multi-decade horizon.
- **Desired retirement income** — a higher target requires a
  proportionally larger portfolio to sustain it through a full retirement.
- **Inflation** — it raises both the income you'll need and every year's
  withdrawal. The Federal Reserve aims for 2% inflation over the longer run;
  the 3% default builds in a margin above that.
- **Life expectancy** — planning to 95 instead of 90 raises the target in
  the example to $3,885,709.26, because the income must last five more years.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Entering desired income in future dollars.** The calculator inflates the
  figure for you. Entering $168,000 when you mean $60,000 in today's money
  would count inflation twice.
- **Contributing less than the full employer match.** A match is
  effectively a guaranteed return. In the example it's the difference between
  running out at 78 and at 84.
- **Using the same return before and after retirement without thinking.**
  Many retirees hold a more conservative mix once withdrawals begin. A lower
  post-retirement return is a more cautious assumption.
- **Planning only to average life expectancy.** An average is just that —
  many people outlive it, and running out of money late in life is hard to
  fix. Test a later age to see how much more you'd need.
- **Treating the target as income replacement only.** The desired income
  figure should cover everything your savings must fund. Subtract expected
  Social Security or pension income from it so the target reflects only what
  the portfolio has to provide.
- **Overlooking contribution limits.** The IRS caps how much you can defer
  into a 401(k) each year — $24,500 for 2026 — so a large planned
  contribution may need to be split across account types.
