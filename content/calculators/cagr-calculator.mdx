When an investment, a business's revenue, or any other value grows over
several years, the path is rarely smooth. Some years are strong, others
flat or negative. The compound annual growth rate, or CAGR, cuts through
that noise. It is the single constant yearly rate that would take the
starting value to the ending value over the same period — one number you
can compare across investments held for different lengths of time.

## How to Use This CAGR Calculator

Enter the beginning value, the ending value, and the length of the period
in years. The period can include a fraction, so an investment held for two
and a half years is entered as 2.5. The calculator returns the CAGR, the
total return over the whole period, the absolute gain or loss in dollars,
and the growth multiple. A chart traces the smoothed path: what the value
would have been at the end of each year if it had grown at exactly the
CAGR the whole time.

The beginning value must be above zero, since there is nothing to grow
from otherwise, and the ending value cannot be negative. An ending value of
zero is allowed and produces a CAGR of −100%.

## How CAGR Is Calculated

CAGR compares only two numbers — where you started and where you ended —
and asks what steady annual rate connects them. Because growth compounds,
the answer is a root of the total growth, not the total divided by the
number of years.

## CAGR Formula

```
CAGR = (Ending value ÷ Beginning value)^(1 ÷ Years) − 1

Total return = Ending value ÷ Beginning value − 1
Value after t years on the smoothed path = Beginning value × (1 + CAGR)^t
```

The ratio of ending to beginning value is the growth multiple. Raising it
to the power of 1 divided by the number of years converts total growth into
an annual rate. The same formula works for fractional years.

## Example Calculation

Say you invested $10,000 and it grew to $25,000 over 7 years.

- **Growth multiple**: $25,000 ÷ $10,000 = 2.5.
- **Total return**: 2.5 − 1 = **150%**, an absolute gain of **$15,000**.
- **CAGR**: 2.5^(1/7) − 1 ≈ 1.1399 − 1 = **13.99% a year**.

On the smoothed path, the investment would be worth $11,398.52 after one
year, $14,809.68 after three years, and exactly $25,000 after seven. Note
that dividing the 150% total return by 7 years gives 21.4% — a figure that
badly overstates the real annual growth because it ignores compounding.

For a fractional period, $10,000 growing to $15,000 over 2.5 years is a 50%
total return and a CAGR of 1.5^(1/2.5) − 1 ≈ **17.61%**.

## CAGR vs. Average Annual Return

A simple average of yearly returns can be misleading when returns vary. If
an investment gains 50% one year and loses 50% the next, the average return
is 0%, yet $10,000 becomes $15,000 and then $7,500 — a 25% loss. The CAGR
over those two years is about −13.4%, which matches what actually happened
to the money. CAGR is always the more honest measure of how a balance grew.

## What CAGR Does and Doesn't Tell You

- **It hides volatility** — two investments with the same CAGR can have had
  very different rides. CAGR says nothing about how far the value fell
  along the way, which matters for risk.
- **It ignores cash flows** — deposits and withdrawals during the period
  distort it. CAGR fits a single lump sum or a metric like revenue, not an
  account you add to every month.
- **It depends on the dates chosen** — starting at a market low or ending
  at a high can make the same investment look very different.
- **It is backward-looking** — past growth rates do not predict future
  returns.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Dividing total return by years** — this ignores compounding and
  overstates the annual rate whenever the investment grew.
- **Using CAGR on an account with regular contributions** — new money makes
  the ending value larger without any actual growth. Use a money-weighted
  return in that case.
- **Ignoring fees, taxes, and inflation** — CAGR on a nominal value before
  costs is higher than what you really kept. Subtract inflation to see real
  growth.
- **Comparing periods of different lengths without annualizing** — total
  return over 3 years and over 10 years are not comparable; CAGR is.
- **Cherry-picking start and end dates** — check the result over several
  periods before drawing conclusions.
